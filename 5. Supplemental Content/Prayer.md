## TL;DR
- *Prayer* is Tim Keller's comprehensive treatment of Christian prayer, drawing on Augustine, Luther, Calvin, and the Psalms to argue that prayer is the primary means by which the soul is transformed and sustained in relationship with God.
- Keller addresses both the mechanics and the heart of prayer: the Lord's Prayer as the irreplaceable model, the twin dangers of formalism and informalism, and the problem of unanswered prayer as a training ground for faith.
- The book argues that prayer is neither a duty we perform to earn God's favor nor a technique to achieve spiritual states, but a conversation with the living God that gradually reshapes the one who prays.

## Key Ideas
- Prayer is best understood as a conversation with God—it requires both speaking and listening, both petition and adoration—and its purpose is not merely to get things but to become the kind of person who can receive what God gives.
- The Lord's Prayer is not one prayer option among many but the master model that organizes all prayer: it begins with adoration, moves through surrender and petition, and ends in doxology.
- Unanswered prayer is not evidence of God's absence but a form of his training: the gap between asking and receiving is where trust, patience, and transformation occur.
- Keller argues that combining structured, liturgical forms of prayer with free, spontaneous speech is the pattern of historic Christian spirituality—each corrects the weaknesses of the other.

## Chapter Summaries
- **Part One: The Necessity of Prayer — Chapter 1: The Necessity of Prayer**
  - **Main Idea:** Prayer is not optional equipment for the serious Christian life but the central activity through which everything else in the Christian life is sustained.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Keller opens by noting that every major Christian tradition—Reformed, Catholic, Wesleyan, charismatic—agrees on the indispensability of prayer even when they disagree on much else.
    - He argues that the great saints of church history are distinguished not by spectacular gifts but by disciplined, deep prayer lives.
    - Prayer is the means by which the grace already given in Christ is appropriated and experienced, not the means of earning it.
    - Neglecting prayer, even while maintaining correct doctrine, results in a dry, powerless faith.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Prayer:** Conversation with God—the soul's primary means of relationship with and dependence upon its Creator and Redeemer.
  - **Takeaway:** The absence of a genuine prayer life is a sign not of busyness but of functional self-reliance, the opposite of faith.

- **Part One: The Necessity of Prayer — Chapter 2: The Touchstone of Prayer**
  - **Main Idea:** The Lord's Prayer, given by Jesus in Matthew 6 and Luke 11, is the touchstone by which all Christian prayer should be evaluated and structured.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Jesus gave the Lord's Prayer not merely as a liturgical formula but as a school of prayer—a model that trains the one who uses it.
    - The prayer begins with God ("Our Father"), not with human need—this order reorients the praying person's entire relationship to their requests.
    - It moves from adoration to submission to petition to forgiveness to deliverance, covering the full range of human need before God.
    - Keller draws on Calvin's teaching that the Lord's Prayer is the "form, pattern, and rule" of all prayer.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Doxology:** A formula of praise to God; the Lord's Prayer ends with one, placing all petitions within a frame of glory.
  - **Takeaway:** Learning to pray means learning to pray this prayer until its shape and its order have reshaped the praying self.

- **Part Two: The Preparation for Prayer — Chapter 3: Tearing Apart the Heavens—Why We Don't Pray**
  - **Main Idea:** Keller examines the spiritual and practical obstacles that keep Christians from praying, arguing that they are mostly symptomatic of unbelief at the level of the heart.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The primary barrier to prayer is not lack of time but a functional belief that we can manage life without God's intervention.
    - Prayerlessness is a form of pride—it assumes we are adequate to handle what we face.
    - Many people pray only in crisis, treating God as a last resort rather than a first resource.
    - Keller notes that even the great theologians Luther and Calvin wrote about the agonizing difficulty of sustained prayer.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Functional atheism:** Believing in God doctrinally while living as though God's active involvement is unnecessary.
  - **Takeaway:** The struggle to pray is not a failure of technique but a diagnosis of where we actually place our trust.

- **Part Two: The Preparation for Prayer — Chapter 4: Adoration—Beginning with God**
  - **Main Idea:** Genuine prayer must begin with adoration—a turning of the whole soul toward God in praise—before proceeding to petition.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Adoration is not flattery; it is an alignment of the self with reality—God is great, and prayer that acknowledges this first is honest prayer.
    - Augustine's model in the *Confessions* begins with praise: "You have made us for yourself, O Lord."
    - Starting with petition rather than adoration produces a distorted prayer life centered on the self.
    - Keller suggests meditating on the character of God—his holiness, love, and power—as a preparation for speaking to him.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Adoration:** The opening movement of prayer in which the soul turns toward God in praise and acknowledgment of who he is.
  - **Takeaway:** Adoration is not preliminary to the real business of prayer; adoration is the foundation that gives every petition its proper shape.

- **Part Two: The Preparation for Prayer — Chapter 5: Meditation—the Missing Connector**
  - **Main Idea:** Meditation on Scripture is the essential bridge between reading the Bible and praying—it is how the word of God descends from the head to the heart.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Keller draws on the Puritan tradition, especially Thomas Watson and Edmund Calamy, for the practice of meditative prayer.
    - Meditation is the practice of dwelling on a text until it begins to speak to the specific conditions of one's life.
    - Without meditation, Bible reading and prayer remain disconnected activities; meditation joins them into a single spiritual act.
    - The Psalms are the biblical model of this practice: they move fluidly between theological statement, lament, and petition.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Meditation:** The practice of dwelling on Scripture slowly and reflectively until it becomes the material and language of prayer.
  - **Takeaway:** Reading Scripture without meditating on it and praying through it is like eating without digesting—the spiritual nutrition remains unavailable.

- **Part Three: The Practice of Prayer — Chapter 6: The Greatest Single Aid to Prayer**
  - **Main Idea:** Keller argues that praying through the Psalms is the greatest single practical aid to a healthy and sustained prayer life.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The Psalms are the divinely given prayer book of the Bible, covering the full range of human experience before God.
    - They authorize and model the expression of negative emotions—grief, anger, fear, desolation—to God, rather than suppressing them.
    - Luther said that in the Psalms you see into the hearts of the saints and find your own experience reflected and sanctified.
    - Praying the Psalms gives language to experiences the pray-er might otherwise not know how to bring to God.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Psalter:** The Book of Psalms as a collected prayerbook and hymnal for the community of faith.
    - **Imprecatory Psalms:** Psalms that invoke God's judgment on enemies; difficult but canonical expressions of the soul's honest cry for justice.
  - **Takeaway:** The Psalms teach us that the range of what can be brought to God in prayer is the full range of human experience—nothing is too dark, too angry, or too despairing to be spoken.

- **Part Three: The Practice of Prayer — Chapter 7: Adoration—Praising**
  - **Main Idea:** Keller develops the practice of adoration more fully, exploring how praise reshapes the affections and reorients the praying person toward God.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Praising God is not a performance for God's benefit but a transformation of the one who praises.
    - C. S. Lewis's insight is cited: we praise what we enjoy, and praise completes the enjoyment.
    - Adoration loosens the grip of anxiety, pride, and self-absorption more effectively than any act of will.
    - Structured liturgical praise—creeds, doxologies, hymns—can sustain adoration when emotion is dry.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Doxology:** A short liturgical expression of praise to God, often concluding prayers or hymns.
  - **Takeaway:** Praise is not optional spiritual decoration; it is the act that reorients the soul's center of gravity from self to God.

- **Part Three: The Practice of Prayer — Chapter 8: Confession—Surrendering**
  - **Main Idea:** Confession is the movement in prayer through which the soul surrenders its pride and self-justification before the God who already knows the truth.
  - **Key Points:**
    - True confession is not merely itemizing sins but acknowledging the deeper condition of the heart from which sins arise.
    - Keller draws on Calvin's insistence that confession must be both specific and rooted in an awareness of the heart's fundamental disorder.
    - The fear of condemnation that makes confession difficult is addressed only by a deep confidence in the gospel—that there is "no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."
    - Confession must move toward assurance of forgiveness, not endless self-examination.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Confession:** The honest acknowledgment before God of one's sins and their root in the disordered heart.
    - **Assurance:** The settled confidence that one is forgiven and accepted in Christ, which must follow confession.
  - **Takeaway:** Confession is not about wallowing in guilt but about the honesty that makes genuine encounter with God possible.

- **Part Three: The Practice of Prayer — Chapter 9: Petition—Asking**
  - **Main Idea:** Petition—asking God for specific things—is the most natural and most problematic part of prayer, and Keller explores its theological foundations and its proper place.
  - **Key Points:**
    - God invites petition throughout Scripture, and Jesus explicitly commands asking, seeking, and knocking.
    - The problem with petition-dominated prayer is that it turns God into a means to human ends rather than recognizing him as the end.
    - Petition that follows adoration and confession has already been shaped by a right understanding of who God is and who we are.
    - Paul's prayers in the epistles are a model: they petition primarily for spiritual goods—wisdom, love, perseverance—before material ones.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Intercession:** Petition made on behalf of others; Keller treats it as a crucial practice of love toward neighbor.
  - **Takeaway:** Asking God for things is not childish or selfish; it is an act of dependence that honors God's fatherhood—but its health depends on what we ask and in what spirit.

- **Part Four: The Problems of Prayer — Chapter 10: Unanswered Prayer—Living with the Mystery**
  - **Main Idea:** Keller addresses the most difficult pastoral problem in prayer: the experience of asking God earnestly for something and not receiving it.
  - **Key Points:**
    - He distinguishes between prayers clearly answered, prayers answered differently than expected, and prayers that appear to receive no answer.
    - Jesus' own prayer in Gethsemane—"not my will but yours"—is the model for holding petition and submission together.
    - Keller draws on C. S. Lewis's *A Grief Observed* and his own pastoral experience with unanswered prayer to acknowledge the genuine pain.
    - The answer to unanswered prayer is not a formula but a deepened trust grounded in the character of God and the cross.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Gethsemane:** The garden where Jesus prayed before his arrest; the paradigm of submitted, anguished petition.
  - **Takeaway:** God's silence in the face of prayer is not indifference but a form of care that exceeds what we can see from within our own timeline.

- **Part Four: The Problems of Prayer — Chapter 11: The Dark Night of the Soul**
  - **Main Idea:** Keller treats the classic mystical experience of spiritual desolation—when prayer feels empty, God feels absent, and spiritual practice produces nothing—as a known and normative phase in Christian growth.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The term "dark night of the soul" comes from John of the Cross; Keller appropriates it for Protestant spiritual theology.
    - Desolation is not necessarily a sign of sin or failure; it may be a form of spiritual weaning from dependence on feelings toward mature faith.
    - The Psalms of lament are the biblical warrant for staying in prayer through desolation rather than abandoning it.
    - Luther's teaching on *Anfechtung*—the assault of doubt and spiritual despair—is cited as an honest Lutheran parallel.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Dark night of the soul:** A period of spiritual dryness, absence, and apparent loss of God's felt presence; a stage in mature spiritual development.
    - **Anfechtung:** Luther's term for the spiritual assault of doubt, despair, and the felt absence of God.
  - **Takeaway:** Continuing to pray through desolation is not self-deception but the deepest form of faith—trust not in feelings but in the God who remains present when he is not felt.

- **Part Five: The Practice of Prayer—Deepening — Chapter 12: Devotional Life**
  - **Main Idea:** Keller outlines a practical, sustainable devotional life integrating Scripture reading, meditation, prayer, and journaling as the daily infrastructure of Christian growth.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Morning is the historical and biblical preference for structured prayer time, beginning the day with reorientation toward God.
    - Keller recommends a rhythm of reading, meditating, praying, and journaling as mutually reinforcing.
    - He addresses the failure of good intentions: starting small and consistent is better than ambitious and erratic.
    - The goal is not performance but relationship—the daily practice is the means, not the end.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Devotional life:** The regular, structured daily practice of Scripture, meditation, and prayer through which faith is nurtured.
  - **Takeaway:** A sustainable devotional life is built by ruthless simplicity and regularity, not by occasional spiritual marathons.
