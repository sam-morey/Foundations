# Nineveh

**Location:** Mosul, Nineveh Governorate, northern Iraq — the ancient city occupied two principal mounds: Kuyunjik (the acropolis, northwest of Mosul) and Nebi Yunus ("Prophet Jonah," under a modern residential neighborhood to the south); GPS ~36.36°N 43.15°E
**Testament:** OT
**Key Themes:** [[Repentance]] · [[Judgment and Restoration]] · [[Sovereignty of God]]

---

## Biblical Significance

Nineveh appears in the biblical story first as a city founded by Nimrod (Genesis 10:11–12), placing it within the table of nations as part of Assyria's founding. Its most dramatic biblical moment is the book of Jonah: God commands the reluctant prophet to preach judgment to Nineveh, and when Jonah finally obeys (after the whale episode), the entire city — from its king to its cattle — repents in sackcloth and ashes, and God relents from the destruction he had planned. This is the only case in the Old Testament of a foreign pagan city responding to an Israelite prophet with mass repentance, and Jesus uses it as the supreme example: "The men of Nineveh will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for they repented at the preaching of Jonah, and behold, something greater than Jonah is here" (Matt 12:41). The "sign of Jonah" — three days in the great fish — becomes Jesus's own sign of death and resurrection.

But Nineveh's repentance was not permanent. Roughly a century after Jonah, the prophet Nahum delivers an extended oracle against the city, describing God's fury against it with vivid, almost terrifying poetry: chariots crashing through streets, shields flashing, the city broken open like a dam, its people scattered. "Woe to the bloody city, all full of lies and plunder" (Nah 3:1). Nineveh had been the nerve center of the Neo-Assyrian Empire's brutal campaigns against Israel (the northern kingdom fell to Assyria in 722 BC) and Judah (Sennacherib besieged Jerusalem in 701 BC). Sennacherib's capital was here; it was from Nineveh that the world's most feared army marched. Its fall in 612 BC to the combined forces of Babylon and the Medes was so complete that the city essentially disappeared from history — so thoroughly that ancient Greek historians debated whether it had ever existed at all. The precision of Nahum's prediction, matched by the archaeological evidence of violent destruction, is one of the most striking examples of fulfilled biblical prophecy in the archaeological record.

## Historical Background

Nineveh was inhabited from at least the seventh millennium BC (Hassuna and Halaf periods), making it one of the oldest continuously occupied sites in the ancient Near East. Its importance grew through the third and second millennia, and it served as a cult center for the goddess Ishtar. The city reached its apex as capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire under Sennacherib (705–681 BC), who dramatically expanded it — he called it the "city without rival" and built the magnificent "Palace without Rival" (*ekal mašarti*) with 71 rooms and approximately 3 km of carved wall reliefs. His son Esarhaddon (681–669 BC) and grandson Ashurbanipal (669–631 BC) continued its development. At its height, Nineveh enclosed approximately 750 hectares within a roughly 12-kilometer circuit of walls with 15 named gates. The city had parks, botanical gardens, and a network of aqueducts — Sennacherib's 50-km Jerwan aqueduct system (featuring the earliest known arch bridge) supplied fresh water from mountain springs.

Ashurbanipal assembled the most extensive library of the ancient world — over 30,000 clay tablets organized by subject and stored in the royal archive. This library included systematic collections of omens, rituals, medical texts, hymns, and literary works accumulated by sending scribes throughout the empire to copy texts. The Epic of Gilgamesh (12 tablets, including Tablet XI's flood story) was preserved in this library. In 612 BC a coalition of Babylonians under Nabopolassar, Medes under Cyaxares, and Scythians laid siege to Nineveh. Ancient sources (the Babylonian Chronicle) record that the Khosr River flooded, breached the walls, and the city fell in a matter of days — a detail that may lie behind Nahum 2:6 ("The river gates are opened; the palace melts away"). The fall was so total that by the time of Xenophon's march through the area in 401 BC (*Anabasis*), the site was uninhabited ruins whose name the locals no longer knew.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Paul Émile Botta (1842–1843, initial probe); Austen Henry Layard, British Museum (1845–1851); Hormuzd Rassam, British Museum (1852–1854, 1878–1882); Leonard King, British Museum (1903–1905); Iraqi State Board of Antiquities (various 20th century); University of California Berkeley / Mosul University collaborative surveys (2012–present, interrupted by ISIS)

- **Layard's excavations (1845–1851):** The foundational excavation of the site. Working under Ottoman permit and British Museum sponsorship, Layard discovered the Southwest Palace (the "Palace without Rival") of Sennacherib on Kuyunjik. He uncovered approximately 71 rooms and corridors, many lined with limestone bas-reliefs depicting military campaigns, hunting scenes, and ritual processions. Among the most significant: the Lachish Reliefs — a series of panels from Room 36 depicting Sennacherib's siege of the Judean city of Lachish in 701 BC with extraordinary detail (siege ramps, archers, deportation of captives, the king on his throne receiving tribute). These panels, running to approximately 21 linear meters, are now displayed in their own gallery in the British Museum (Room 10b). Layard also discovered many Assyrian colossal winged bull-men (*lamassu*) guardian figures, now divided between the British Museum and the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York.
- **Ashurbanipal's Library (Rassam, 1852–1854):** Hormuzd Rassam, Layard's assistant and successor (himself a native of Mosul), discovered the North Palace of Ashurbanipal and within it the royal library — approximately 20,000–30,000 clay tablet fragments, representing thousands of individual texts. The tablets, baked hard by the fire that destroyed the palace in 612 BC, were paradoxically preserved by the very destruction. They included the 12-tablet Epic of Gilgamesh (Tablet XI contains the flood narrative, published by George Smith in 1872, causing a sensation in Victorian England), creation myths, astronomical and astrological texts, and administrative records. The collection is housed at the British Museum (the "Kuyunjik Collection").
- **Taylor Prism / Sennacherib's Prism (acquired 1830, Colonel Taylor):** A six-sided clay prism, approximately 38 cm tall, inscribed in Akkadian with Sennacherib's annals for eight military campaigns. In the account of the third campaign (701 BC), it describes his invasion of Judah: "As for Hezekiah the Judean, who had not submitted to my yoke, I shut up 46 of his strong walled cities... As for him, I shut him up like a bird in a cage in Jerusalem, his royal city." The text notably does not claim to have captured Jerusalem — consistent with the biblical account (2 Kgs 19:35–36) that the Assyrian army withdrew after an angel of the Lord killed 185,000 soldiers. The Taylor Prism is housed in the British Museum (BM 91032). A nearly identical prism (the Oriental Institute Prism, also called the Chicago Prism) is in the Oriental Institute, University of Chicago.
- **Nebi Yunus excavations (limited access due to mosque and housing above):** The southern mound, containing the Arsenal (*ekal mašarti*) of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal, has been excavated only partially due to a mosque and dense housing above. Tunnels dug by ISIS looters (2014–2016) inadvertently revealed Esarhaddon's palace inscription beneath the mosque and massive Assyrian sculptural remains. Iraqi archaeologists documented some ISIS tunnel finds in 2016–2017.
- **ISIS destruction (2014–2015):** ISIS fighters demolished the Mosul Museum's Assyrian galleries, smashed the Nergal Gate lamassu guardians, bulldozed portions of the Kuyunjik mound, and systematically looted high-value artifacts for sale. The loss included several intact room-length relief sequences. Reconstruction and re-excavation efforts are ongoing.
