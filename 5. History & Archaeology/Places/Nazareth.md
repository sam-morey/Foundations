**Testament:** NT (Jesus's hometown)

## Biblical Significance

Nazareth holds a paradoxical place in the Gospels: it is where Jesus spent the vast majority of his earthly life — roughly thirty years — yet it is largely silent in his story until he begins his public ministry. Luke 2:51–52 summarizes those decades in two verses: Jesus "went down with them and came to Nazareth and was submissive to them... And Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man." The Gospels describe him as "Jesus of Nazareth" more frequently than any other title except "Jesus Christ," suggesting the town's name was inseparable from his identity in the minds of his contemporaries. The angel Gabriel announces to Mary in Nazareth (Luke 1:26–38), and Joseph and Mary return there from Egypt after the flight (Matthew 2:23, citing a fulfillment of prophetic themes — "He shall be called a Nazarene").

The dramatic synagogue scene in Luke 4:14–30 is Nazareth's defining New Testament moment. Jesus reads from Isaiah 61 ("The Spirit of the Lord is upon me...") and declares it fulfilled in their hearing. The congregation's initial wonder turns to rage when he implies the blessings of the kingdom will extend beyond Israel, and they attempt to throw him off a cliff. Jesus's observation — "no prophet is acceptable in his hometown" — sets Nazareth as the paradigm of unbelief and rejection that the Gospel of Luke will trace across the entire narrative. John 1:46's skeptical "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" reflects the town's low reputation even within Galilee — a small, agriculturally-dependent village with no mention in the Old Testament, Josephus, or the Talmud, leading some 19th-20th century scholars to doubt its existence during the first century. Archaeology has now resolved that question conclusively.

## Historical Background

Nazareth is entirely absent from the Old Testament, from the writings of Josephus (who lists numerous Galilean towns but not Nazareth), and from the Mishnah and Talmud — a silence that led some 19th-century critics (notably Bruno Bauer) to argue the town did not exist in the first century and that the Gospels' setting was fictional. This "mythicist" position has been comprehensively refuted by archaeological evidence, particularly from excavations since the 1990s.

First-century Nazareth was a small agricultural village of approximately 400–500 inhabitants, perched on a limestone ridge in Lower Galilee. Its economy was based on grain agriculture, olives, and viticulture, with agricultural installations (presses, silos, cisterns) being the primary physical features of the settlement. The town had no significant political or commercial role — it sat off the main trade routes and had no monumental architecture — which explains both its absence from literary sources and the stigma attached to the name ("Can anything good come from Nazareth?"). The nearest city was Sepphoris (~6 km away), a major Herodian urban center under construction during Jesus's youth; scholars have speculated that Joseph and Jesus may have worked there as builders (the Greek word tekton, translated "carpenter," covers stone and general construction work as well as woodworking).

After the Bar Kokhba revolt (135 AD), a Jewish priestly family group (mishmar) relocated to Nazareth, and the Nazareth Inscription — an imperial edict about tomb disturbance — suggests the town attracted imperial attention sometime in the 1st–2nd century AD. By the Byzantine period Nazareth had become a Christian pilgrimage destination, with churches built over sites associated with the Annunciation and Mary's well.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Father Bellarmino Bagatti (Franciscan, 1955–1969; excavated beneath the Church of the Annunciation); Yardenna Alexandre (Israel Antiquities Authority, 2009–2010, salvage excavation of a first-century house); Yigal Tepper and Yotam Tepper (surveys and agricultural installations); Ruth Kohn (IAA, ongoing salvage excavations in the city)

- **Church of the Annunciation (Bagatti, 1955–1969):** Excavations beneath the modern Basilica of the Annunciation (completed 1969) revealed a sequence of structures over a first-century residential site: Byzantine mosaics and column bases, a Crusader chapel, and beneath these, rock-cut installations (cisterns, a grain silo, a pit) consistent with 1st-century domestic use. Bagatti identified the site as the location of Mary's home based on the tradition preserved in Byzantine-era pilgrimage accounts. Significant finds included a 3rd–4th century AD Greek graffito on a plaster fragment reading "Χαιρε Μαρια" (Hail Mary), suggesting early veneration of the site.
- **First-Century Courtyard House (Yardenna Alexandre, IAA, 2009–2010):** During salvage excavation prior to construction of a new education center in central Nazareth, Alexandre's team uncovered a well-preserved first-century AD residential structure — the first clearly identified domestic building from Jesus's period to be excavated in Nazareth. The house consisted of two rooms with a courtyard, rock-cut storage pits, and pottery consistent with a Jewish household (chalk stone vessels, indicating purity concerns). The ceramics, coins, and stratigraphy confirmed first-century occupation. The IAA announced this as "the first dwelling from the Jesus period to be uncovered in Nazareth." The house is now partially preserved beneath the Sisters of Nazareth convent site.
- **Agricultural Installations:** Multiple surveys and salvage excavations have identified first-century agricultural rock-cut features throughout Nazareth: olive presses (the crushing stone and press bed), grain silos (bell-shaped pits cut into bedrock), wine presses, and cisterns. These confirm the small-scale agricultural character of the settlement consistent with the Gospel picture.
- **Nazareth Village:** A reconstruction project adjacent to the original site has used archaeological data (from Bagatti, Alexandre, and survey work) to recreate a first-century Galilean village with period-accurate architecture, terracing, and agricultural features. While not an excavation site itself, it draws on excavated evidence from the immediate area.
- **Nazareth Inscription (discovered ~1878, acquired by the Bibliothèque nationale de France):** A marble slab (~60 × 38 cm) bearing a Greek inscription of an imperial edict ordering that tombs not be disturbed, bodies not be removed, and violators be punished with death — unusually harsh penalties for this type of ordinance. The inscription was reportedly purchased from an antiquities dealer who acquired it in Nazareth. The emperor who issued it is debated (Augustus, Tiberius, and Claudius are the main candidates; Claudius is currently favored). Some scholars have proposed the edict was a response to rumors circulating in the empire — possibly including reports of the resurrection of Jesus — though this connection is speculative. The inscription is held in the Cabinet des Médailles at the Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris.
- **Mary's Well / Greek Orthodox Church of the Annunciation:** The spring-fed well in the center of ancient Nazareth (the only reliable water source in the village) is identified by the Greek Orthodox tradition as the site of the Annunciation (Gabriel appearing to Mary at the well, drawing on an early tradition found in the Protoevangelium of James). A Byzantine church and Crusader structure preceded the current 1750s-era church. The well's water channel has been traced archaeologically and confirmed as ancient.
