# Capernaum

**Location:** Tel Hum, northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee, Galilee region, Israel — approximately 3 km northeast of Tabgha
**Testament:** NT
**Key Themes:** [[Kingdom of God]] · [[Miracles of Jesus]] · [[Discipleship]]

---

## Biblical Significance

Capernaum served as Jesus's operational headquarters throughout his Galilean ministry. After being rejected at Nazareth, Jesus relocated here and Matthew records it as "his own city" (Matt 9:1), fulfilling Isaiah's prophecy about light coming to "the way of the sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the Gentiles" (Matt 4:15–16). From this base he delivered some of his most significant miracles: healing a demoniac in the synagogue (Mark 1:21–28), restoring Peter's mother-in-law of fever (Mark 1:29–31), healing the paralytic lowered through the roof (Mark 2:1–12), and raising Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:21–43). He also called Matthew/Levi from the tax collector's booth located here (Matt 9:9) and healed the centurion's servant without entering the Roman soldier's house — a defining moment of faith crossing ethnic boundaries (Matt 8:5–13).

Yet Capernaum is also the site of one of Jesus's sharpest warnings. Despite witnessing more miracles than almost any other location, the city refused to repent, and Jesus pronounced: "And you, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven? You will be brought down to Hades" (Matt 11:23). The Bread of Life discourse in John 6 — Jesus's most theologically dense teaching on himself as the true manna — was delivered in the Capernaum synagogue (John 6:59), and it resulted in many disciples turning away. The city thus represents both the fullness of Jesus's presence and the tragedy of unbelief in the face of overwhelming evidence.

## Historical Background

Capernaum (Hebrew: *Kfar Nahum*, "village of Nahum") was a prosperous fishing village situated on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee at a key point on the Via Maris, the ancient trade road linking Damascus to Egypt. In the first century AD it had a population estimated between 1,500 and 1,700 people. The town's economy centered on fishing in the lake — the abundant catches of tilapia, sardines, and catfish were salted and exported across the region — along with customs collection at a toll station on the major road. Zebedee's fishing business, which employed hired workers (Mark 1:20), reflects the commercial scale of the local fishing industry.

A Roman military garrison was stationed nearby, attested by the centurion of Matthew 8 who commissioned and paid for the construction of the local synagogue — a remarkable act of patronage by a Gentile toward a Jewish community (Luke 7:5). The town was not heavily fortified and sat within the tetrarchy of Herod Antipas, who administered Galilee and Perea. After the fall of Jerusalem in 70 AD, Jewish Christians and later Jewish communities continued to inhabit Capernaum into the Byzantine period, making it a site of layered religious occupation for centuries.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Virgilio Corbo OFM and Stanislao Loffreda OFM, Franciscan Custody of the Holy Land (1968–1986); Vassilios Tzaferis, Israel Antiquities Authority (1978–1982, the eastern insula)

The site of Tel Hum was first identified as Capernaum by Edward Robinson in 1838. Systematic excavations began under the Franciscans in 1968 after they acquired the western portion of the site in the late nineteenth century. The Israeli excavations covered the eastern residential quarters concurrently.

- **White Limestone Synagogue (4th–5th century AD):** A well-preserved synagogue with carved reliefs — menorahs, eagles, palm fronds — sits prominently on the site. Excavations beneath its basalt stone foundation revealed an earlier basalt floor and walls dated to the first century AD, widely identified as the synagogue where Jesus taught (Mark 1:21; John 6:59). The first-century structure appears to have been enlarged and rebuilt in the later Roman period.
- **"Peter's House" (Insula Sacra):** Beneath the modern octagonal Franciscan church, Corbo excavated a first-century residential *insula* (a cluster of interconnected rooms around courtyards). One room — larger than the others, with a plastered floor — shows evidence of repeated replastering and a change from domestic to communal use beginning in the mid-first century. The walls bore over 130 graffiti in Aramaic, Greek, Syriac, and Latin, including references to "Peter," "Lord Jesus Christ," and a possible fish/boat symbol. By the late fourth century, the room had been enclosed within a larger octagonal Byzantine church — the same architectural form used to mark sacred memorial sites elsewhere (e.g., the Church of the Nativity, Bethlehem). This is the strongest candidate for the "house of Simon and Andrew" mentioned in Mark 1:29.
- **Basalt Millstones and Fishing Equipment:** Excavations across the residential quarters recovered basalt grinding stones, fishhooks, net weights (lead sinkers), and storage vessels, confirming the village's fishing and agricultural economy.
- **First-Century Ceramics:** Domestic pottery, oil lamps, and cooking vessels from Stratum IV (late Second Temple period, roughly 100 BC–70 AD) are consistent with the Gospel narratives' first-century setting.
