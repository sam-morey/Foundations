# Hazor

**Location:** Tel Hazor, Upper Galilee, northern Israel — approximately 14 km north of the Sea of Galilee and 10 km north of Rosh Pinna; 33.0175° N, 35.5679° E; the tell rises over the surrounding basalt plain
**Testament:** OT primarily
**Key Themes:** [[Conquest]] · [[Kingdom of Israel]] · [[Covenant]] · [[Judgment]]

---

## Biblical Significance

Hazor is introduced in the conquest narrative as the greatest of the Canaanite cities — "the head of all those kingdoms" (Joshua 11:10). Its king Jabin assembled a vast coalition of northern Canaanite kings, with horses and chariots, to oppose Israel at the Waters of Merom; Joshua routed them, pursued the coalition, and "burned Hazor with fire" (Joshua 11:11). The destruction of Hazor is described with more emphasis than any other conquest — it alone among the northern cities is burned, a detail that corresponds to an archaeologically attested destruction layer and has made Hazor the most debated site in the conquest historicity discussion.

Hazor reappears in Judges 4 where another Jabin, king of Hazor, oppresses Israel for twenty years — suggesting the site was rebuilt and the royal name perpetuated — before Deborah and Barak defeat his general Sisera. Solomon later rebuilds and fortifies Hazor as one of his key chariot cities (1 Kings 9:15), alongside Megiddo and Gezer — the same trio that have yielded similar six-chambered gates and casemate wall systems. Hazor's final biblical mention is its fall to the Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser III in 732 BC (2 Kings 15:29), which ends Israelite control of the northern Galilee. The site's importance is also reflected in extra-biblical sources: Hazor is the only Canaanite city in the southern Levant to appear in the Mari royal archive letters (18th century BC) as a destination for merchants and diplomatic envoys, confirming its extraordinary regional dominance well before the Israelite period.

## Historical Background

Tel Hazor consists of two distinct areas: an upper mound (the tel proper, ~12 acres) and a vast lower city enclosed by earthwork ramparts (~175 acres), giving it a total settled area of approximately 200 acres — by far the largest city in Late Bronze Age Canaan, with a population estimated at 15,000–20,000. This scale dwarfs contemporary cities; even Jerusalem in the same period was a fraction of its size. The lower city was occupied only during the Middle and Late Bronze Ages (~1750–1200 BC); its abandonment in the Iron Age reduced the site to the upper mound alone.

The city appears in the Egyptian Execration Texts (early 2nd millennium BC) as a known polity, in the Mari Letters (~1800 BC) as a trading partner receiving tin and textiles from Mesopotamia, and in the Amarna Letters (~1350 BC) as an active Canaanite city-state. An Egyptian list from the reign of Thutmose III (15th century BC) also names it. Hazor's position on the major trade and military route from Egypt to Syria and Mesopotamia — the same Via Maris that passes Megiddo — gave it commercial and strategic dominance over the northern Levant. The city's Late Bronze Age destruction (~1230–1200 BC, Stratum XIII) is one of the most dramatic in the archaeological record: massive burning, smashed cult objects, and deliberate mutilation of royal statuary suggest not a gradual decline but a violent conquest.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** John Garstang (1928, initial sounding); Yigael Yadin (1955–1958, 1968, Hebrew University / Israel Exploration Society); Amnon Ben-Tor (1990–present, Hebrew University — the ongoing Selz Foundation Hazor Excavations)

- **Stratigraphy Overview:** Yadin identified 21 strata on the upper mound and an additional 3 in the lower city, spanning the Chalcolithic through the Hellenistic period. Ben-Tor's ongoing work has refined and extended this sequence; the site now has one of the most precisely established stratigraphic columns in the Levant.
- **Lower City Canaanite Temples (Strata 2–1B of the Lower City):** Yadin excavated a series of superimposed temples in the lower city, including a Late Bronze Age "orthostat temple" with basalt orthostats lining the walls, a niche for a cult statue, and a raised holy-of-holies — a layout strikingly similar to the tripartite plan of Solomon's Temple described in 1 Kings 6. Finds included a basalt orthostat carved with two hands raised in prayer, a basalt statue of a seated deity, lion-headed drinking vessels, and incense stands.
- **Stelae Temple (Area C, Lower City):** A small temple containing a row of basalt standing stones (masseboth) of varying heights, and a small statue of a seated figure with its hands raised, and a figurine of a crouching lioness. The collection of stelae may represent ancestor veneration or divine presence markers. Now in the Israel Museum.
- **Late Bronze Age Destruction Layer (Stratum XIII, ~1230–1200 BC):** Yadin found clear evidence of a catastrophic burning and destruction throughout the lower city — collapsed mud-brick walls, burned wooden beams, smashed pottery, and (critically) deliberately decapitated and mutilated basalt statues of Canaanite deities and rulers. The deliberate nature of the destruction — particularly the targeted mutilation of cult images — suggests ideological motivation beyond mere conquest. Yadin attributed this to the Israelites; Ben-Tor continues to defend this attribution against alternative proposals (Sea Peoples, internal revolt, other Canaanite enemies).
- **Solomonic Six-Chambered Gate (Stratum X):** Identified by Yadin based on 1 Kings 9:15, a six-chambered gate (three chambers per side) with a nearly identical plan to the Solomonic gates at Megiddo and Gezer. Yadin famously read 1 Kings 9:15 and then predicted where such a gate would be found at Hazor — and found it. The gate and associated casemate wall form the clearest physical expression of the Solomonic building program attested archaeologically.
- **Israelite Water Shaft System (Stratum VIII, ~9th century BC):** Ahab-period engineers cut a massive shaft (~30 m deep, ~16 m × 16 m in plan) through the bedrock of the upper mound, reaching the water table. A plastered staircase spirals down the shaft walls. This is among the most ambitious hydraulic engineering projects of Iron Age Israel, comparable to those at Megiddo and Gezer. The shaft was fully excavated in Ben-Tor's expedition.
- **Cuneiform Tablet Fragments (2010, Stratum XIV–XV):** During Ben-Tor's excavations, fragments of cuneiform tablets were discovered in a LB palace context — the first cuneiform writing found in an Israelite excavation context at Hazor. The fragments suggest Hazor had a scribal and administrative tradition in the Akkadian language, consistent with its depiction in the Mari and Amarna archives.
- **Ahab-Period Palace (Stratum VIII–VII):** A large ashlar-built administrative palace attributed to the Omride dynasty, with proto-Ionic pilaster capitals — part of the ambitious monumental building program documented also at Megiddo and Samaria.
