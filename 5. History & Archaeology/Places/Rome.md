# Rome

**Location:** Rome, Lazio, Italy — the biblical sites cluster around the Forum Romanum, Palatine Hill, Capitoline Hill, the Appian Way (Via Appia), and the catacombs of the Via Appia corridor; GPS ~41.89°N 12.49°E
**Testament:** NT
**Key Themes:** [[Sovereignty of God]] · [[Suffering and Persecution]] · [[The Gospel]]

---

## Biblical Significance

Rome stands at the horizon of Paul's entire apostolic calling. From early in his ministry, Paul conceived of his mission as reaching "from Jerusalem and all the way around to Illyricum" and ultimately to Spain — with Rome as the necessary midpoint (Romans 15:19–24). He wrote the letter to the Romans before he had ever visited the city, producing in it his most systematic presentation of the gospel: the universal human condition under sin (1:18–3:20), justification by faith alone (3:21–5:21), union with Christ and life in the Spirit (6–8), the mystery of Israel's place in salvation history (9–11), and the shape of life in the new community (12–16). He sent it ahead as a theological introduction and a request for support in his planned mission to Spain. The Roman church, which Paul did not found (it likely originated through Jewish pilgrims converted at Pentecost or through other early witnesses), was already well-established by the time he wrote.

Acts ends — deliberately and arrestingly — in Rome. Paul arrives under guard after his harrowing voyage and shipwreck (Acts 27–28), walks the last miles of the Appian Way to be met by Roman Christians who had come out to greet him as far as the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns (Acts 28:15 — "when Paul saw them, he thanked God and took courage"), and spends two years under house arrest. The book ends not with Paul's death but with a vision of the gospel advancing unhindered: "He lived there two whole years at his own expense, and welcomed all who came to him, proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness and without hindrance" (Acts 28:30–31). The last Greek word of Acts is *akolutos* — "without hindrance." Paul was almost certainly executed in Rome under Nero, probably around 64–67 AD. Peter also died in Rome, according to unbroken early tradition (Clement of Rome, Ignatius of Antioch, Irenaeus), and the words of John 21:18–19 — "when you are old... another will carry you where you do not want to go" — were understood from earliest Christian interpretation as a prediction of Peter's crucifixion. Rome is thus the place where both pillars of the apostolic church shed their blood, and 1 Peter 5:13's "she who is at Babylon" is almost universally understood as a reference to Rome.

## Historical Background

Rome's legendary founding date of 753 BC (by Romulus and Remus, according to tradition) marks the beginning of an extraordinary trajectory. The Roman Republic (509–27 BC) expanded Roman power through Italy, then across the Mediterranean world, absorbing Greece (146 BC), the eastern Mediterranean, North Africa, Gaul, Britain, and ultimately the entire world Paul knew. Julius Caesar's assassination (44 BC) and the subsequent civil wars ended with Augustus (Octavian) becoming the first emperor in 27 BC, inaugurating the Pax Romana — a period of relative peace across the Mediterranean world that lasted roughly 200 years. This Pax Romana, the Roman road network (over 80,000 km of paved roads by the 2nd century AD), the common language of Greek in the eastern empire, and Roman law's framework of citizenship — Paul exploited all of these for his missionary strategy.

At its height under Trajan (98–117 AD), Rome's population may have reached one million, making it the largest city in the ancient Western world. The city was divided into 14 administrative regions, featured aqueducts supplying over one billion liters of water daily, monumental public buildings (Forum of Augustus, Pantheon, Colosseum), and a multi-ethnic population including a substantial Jewish community. Jews had been expelled from Rome by Claudius around 49 AD (Acts 18:2; confirmed by Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4: "He expelled the Jews from Rome since they were constantly making disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus" — most scholars read "Chrestus" as a garbled reference to "Christus," suggesting the expulsion was triggered by disputes between Jews and Jewish Christians). Claudius's death in 54 AD allowed Jewish Christians, including Aquila and Priscilla, to return — explaining the multi-ethnic composition of the Roman church Paul addresses in Romans 14–15.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Numerous excavation campaigns from the Renaissance onward; major systematic excavations by the Soprintendenza Speciale per i Beni Archeologici di Roma; ongoing work by multiple Italian, European, and American institutions

- **Mamertine Prison (*Carcer Tullianum*, Forum area):** The ancient Roman state prison (career), hewn from living rock near the Capitoline Hill at the north end of the Forum Romanum, consists of two chambers: an upper room (the *carcer* proper) and a lower, circular underground chamber (*tullianum*) accessible only through a hole in the ceiling, used for executing or incarcerating high-value prisoners. The structure dates to at least the 4th century BC (the tufa masonry) and was in active use through the Roman Republic and Empire. Historical sources record that Jugurtha, Vercingetorix, and the Catilinarian conspirators were held or executed here. The tradition that Peter and Paul were imprisoned here is early (an inscription in the upper church, *San Pietro in Carcere*, dates the association to late antiquity) but unverifiable archaeologically — however, the building is certainly a genuine Roman prison of the period and class that held high-profile captives. A later Roman legend held that Peter, fettered here, miraculously produced a spring of water (still pointed out to visitors) to baptize his prison guards.
- **Via Appia (Appian Way):** The *regina viarum* ("queen of roads"), begun by the censor Appius Claudius Caecus in 312 BC, ran 540 km from Rome southeast to Brundisium (modern Brindisi). The road entered Rome from the southeast — the direction from which Paul would have traveled from Puteoli (Acts 28:13–14). The biblical reference to the "Forum of Appius" (about 69 km from Rome) and "Three Taverns" (about 48 km from Rome) as meeting points (Acts 28:15) matches precisely the road stations known from the *Tabula Peutingeriana* (an ancient Roman road map). The extant paving stones of the Via Appia Antica south of Rome are among the best-preserved Roman road surfaces in the world.
- **Christian Catacombs of the Via Appia corridor:** Begun in the late 2nd and early 3rd centuries AD, the catacombs were underground burial networks (not hiding places, as popular tradition claims) used by Christian communities who preferred burial to cremation. The Catacomb of Callixtus (*Catacombe di San Callisto*, Via Appia Antica 110) is the most archaeologically significant: it contains the Crypt of the Popes, with the burial niches of at least 9 second and third-century bishops of Rome (including Pontian and Sixtus II), as well as the Crypt of Santa Cecilia. The Catacomb of Domitilla (*Catacombe di Domitilla*) nearby is the oldest and largest in Rome, with over 17 km of galleries; it contains the earliest surviving pictorial image of Christ as the Good Shepherd (3rd century fresco, still in situ). The Catacomb of Priscilla (*Catacombe di Priscilla*, Via Salaria) contains what may be the earliest known image of the Virgin Mary (3rd century, the "Orant Fresco" in the Greek Chapel).
- **Arch of Titus (Forum Romanum, completed 81 AD):** Dedicated to the Emperor Titus after his death by his brother Domitian, the Arch of Titus commemorates Titus's sack of Jerusalem in 70 AD and the destruction of the Second Temple. The interior relief panels — among the finest Roman sculptural reliefs surviving — show Roman soldiers carrying the spoils of the Jerusalem temple through the streets of Rome: the golden seven-branched menorah, the silver trumpets, and the table of showbread. The menorah panel is one of the most important depictions of Second Temple cult objects in existence and has been the subject of intense scholarly and Jewish community interest regarding the menorah's ultimate fate. The arch stood as a reminder, visible to all who walked the Via Sacra, that Rome had crushed Jerusalem — but within two generations, the community Rome had tried to destroy would be the dominant religious force shaping its civilization.
- **San Clemente Basilica (Via di San Giovanni in Laterano):** A remarkable three-layer site. The present basilica (c. 1100 AD) sits atop a 4th-century basilica, which in turn sits atop a first-century *insula* (apartment/commercial building) alongside a *mithraeum* (Mithraic temple, late 1st–2nd century). The first-century *insula* is one of the best-preserved examples of Roman domestic/commercial architecture in the city. Early tradition associated the site with the house of Clement, the third bishop of Rome and author of 1 Clement (c. 96 AD), who may be the Clement named in Philippians 4:3. The excavations (conducted by Dominican friars of the Irish Province, 1857–1870, and ongoing) reveal how layers of Christian community overlaid the ordinary Roman urban fabric.
- **Colosseum (*Amphitheatrum Flavium*, opened 80 AD under Titus):** Built by Vespasian (begun c. 70 AD using spoils from the Jewish War, including labor from Jewish prisoners) and completed by Titus. Seating approximately 50,000–80,000 spectators, it hosted gladiatorial games, animal hunts, and public executions. Whether Christian martyrs were executed *in* the Colosseum specifically is debated by historians (direct ancient evidence is thin), but the building is undeniably the architectural symbol of the imperial power that persecuted early Christians. The cross standing in the arena today is a modern commemoration.
- **Vatican Necropolis / Tomb of Peter (St. Peter's Basilica):** Excavations beneath St. Peter's Basilica commissioned by Pope Pius XII (1940–1949) revealed a Roman necropolis (cemetery) beneath the basilica, and at the center, under the main altar, a structure identified as the *tropaion* (trophy/memorial) of Peter mentioned by the Roman presbyter Gaius around 200 AD. A repository of bones found in a niche associated with the *tropaion*, labeled in Greek "Peter is here" (*Petros eni*), is taken by Catholic tradition as the bones of the apostle. The archaeologist Margherita Guarducci published the key analysis of the Greek inscription in the 1960s. The necropolis beneath St. Peter's can be visited by appointment.
