## Biblical Significance

Bethany is the village of close friendship. Among all the places Jesus visited, Bethany stands apart as the home of Mary, Martha, and Lazarus — a family in which Jesus found a particular depth of welcome, rest, and love. John 11:5 states it simply and memorably: "Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus." This is not the generic love of a teacher for his students but the personal, particular love of a friend for a household he returned to again and again. In the crowded, politically dangerous final weeks of Jesus's ministry, Bethany was his retreat — close enough to Jerusalem to reach on foot, far enough to escape the temple authorities.

The raising of Lazarus, recounted in [[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 11|John 11]], is the climactic sign of John's Gospel and the immediate precipitant of Jesus's arrest. Lazarus has been dead four days — decomposition has begun, as Martha notes ("Lord, by this time there is an odor," [[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 11|John 11:39]]). The four-day detail is theologically significant: Jewish tradition held that the soul hovered near the body for three days before departing; four days means the death is unambiguous and irreversible. When Jesus calls Lazarus out of the tomb, it is a claim to be "the resurrection and the life" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 11|John 11:25]]) — a statement he makes not in the abstract but moments before proving it.

The response to the Lazarus miracle is the gospel's great irony: the Jewish authorities convene urgently precisely because the miracle is undeniable. "What are we to do? For this man performs many signs. If we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 11|John 11:47–48]]). Caiaphas's cynical verdict — "it is better for you that one man should die for the people" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 11|John 11:50]]) — becomes John's great theological irony: the very act intended to prevent the kingdom brings it about.

Six days before Passover, Jesus returns to Bethany for a dinner at which Mary performs the most extravagant act of devotion in the Gospels: she takes a pound of expensive ointment of pure nard, anoints Jesus's feet, and wipes them with her hair ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 12|John 12:1–8]]). The house is filled with the fragrance. Judas objects — "Why was this ointment not sold for three hundred denarii and given to the poor?" — but Jesus defends Mary: "She has done it to prepare me for burial. Truly, I say to you, wherever this gospel is proclaimed in the whole world, what she has done will also be told in memory of her" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/40. Matthew/Matthew 26|Matt 26:13]]). The anointing at Bethany is the last act of personal worship before the Passion.

Luke's Gospel records an earlier Bethany scene — the famous story of Mary sitting at Jesus's feet while Martha bustles with preparations and resents it ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/42. Luke/Luke 10|Luke 10:38–42]]). Jesus's reply — "Mary has chosen the good portion, which will not be taken away from her" — is not a rebuke of service but a correction of anxious, resentful service. The Bethany home becomes Luke's paradigm for the proper ordering of hearing and doing.

Finally, Luke places the Ascension near Bethany. Jesus led the disciples "out as far as Bethany, and lifting up his hands he blessed them. While he blessed them, he parted from them and was carried up into heaven" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/42. Luke/Luke 24|Luke 24:50–51]]). The last place Jesus's feet touched the earth before his bodily exaltation was the path toward the village of his closest friends.

## Historical Background

In the first century, Bethany was a small agricultural village on the Jericho road, the main route connecting Jerusalem to the Jordan Valley and the Transjordan. It sat just over the ridge of the Mount of Olives from Jerusalem, placing it approximately a "Sabbath day's journey" from the city ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/44. Acts/Acts 1|Acts 1:12]] uses this term for the Mount of Olives summit, somewhat further). The Jericho road was one of the most traveled roads in Judea, making Bethany a village through which pilgrims, traders, and armies regularly passed.

The modern town of al-Eizariya preserves both the ancient name's meaning and a continuous tradition of the site's association with Lazarus. The Arabic name derives from the Greek "Lazarion" — meaning "place of Lazarus" — demonstrating that the identification of this site with the Lazarus family was established by at least the Byzantine period and embedded itself into the place-name used by Arab-speaking inhabitants after the Islamic conquest.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Tomb of Lazarus:**

The traditional Tomb of Lazarus has been venerated in Bethany since at least the 4th century AD. The Byzantine pilgrim Egeria (~381 AD) visited the site and described a church there. The current access to the tomb is through a doorway in the side of the hill, down 24 stone steps into a small rock-cut chamber. The tomb itself is not a 1st-century kokhim-style tomb (with long horizontal niches) but rather a vaulted chamber; its identification as the specific tomb of Lazarus is based on continuous tradition rather than archaeological typing.

**Byzantine and Crusader Churches:**

- A **Byzantine church** was built over the traditional tomb site in the 4th century; Egeria describes it, and the 6th-century Madaba Map (a Byzantine mosaic floor map of the Holy Land, found in Jordan) depicts Bethany with a church labelled "the place of Lazarus."
- A **Crusader church** was built over or adjacent to the Byzantine structure in the 12th century.
- The current **Greek Orthodox Church** preserves portions of the Crusader and Byzantine remains.
- A **Franciscan Church of St. Lazarus** was built in 1954 to serve Western pilgrims, designed by Antonio Barluzzi in a modernist style with mosaic depictions of the Lazarus story.

**Excavations in the village:**

Archaeological surveys of al-Eizariya have identified pottery and ceramic evidence from the Second Temple period (1st century BC – 1st century AD), confirming occupation of the site during the time of Jesus's ministry. The evidence is consistent with a small agricultural village of the period.
