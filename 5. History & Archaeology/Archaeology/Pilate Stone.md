**Type:** Limestone dedication inscription (Latin)
**Date:** ~26–37 AD (during Pilate's tenure as prefect)
**Discovered:** 1961, Caesarea Maritima — during excavations of the theater complex by Italian archaeologist Antonio Frova
**Current Location:** Israel Museum, Jerusalem (original); replica displayed in situ at Caesarea Maritima
**Biblical Connection:** The Passion narrative; all four Gospels; Acts 3–4; 1 Timothy 6:13
**Key Themes:** [[Sacrifice & Atonement]] · [[Messiah & Christ]] · [[Redemption & Salvation]]

## The Discovery

In 1961, an Italian archaeological team led by Antonio Frova was excavating the theater at Caesarea Maritima — the Roman administrative capital of Judaea built by Herod the Great on the Mediterranean coast. Working in the theater's seating area, excavators found a limestone block measuring approximately 82 × 65 cm that had been reused as a step in a stairway renovation from the 4th century AD. The block had originally been carved as an architectural inscription and had been repurposed — face down — as building material, which had paradoxically preserved its inscription surface.

The Latin inscription was partially damaged, with several letters missing or eroded, but enough survived to read clearly. When cleaned and studied, it bore four lines of Latin text — the most important Latin inscription ever found in Israel. The discovery was announced and immediately recognized as one of the most significant archaeological finds related to New Testament history.

---

## What It Says

The four-line Latin inscription, with lacunae (gaps) indicated by brackets, reads:

```
[DIS AUGUST]IS TIBERIÉUM
[...PON]TIVS PILATVS
[...PRAEF]ECTVS IVDAÉ[AE]
[...FECIT D]E[DICAVIT]
```

Reconstructed in full:

> "[In honor of the divine Augustus,] a Tiberieum [Pontius Pilate, Prefect of Judaea, made and dedicated this]"

The "Tiberieum" was a public building — possibly a lighthouse or a temple — dedicated to the honor of the Emperor Tiberius (who reigned 14–37 AD). The key elements are:

- **PONTIVS PILATVS** — the full name "Pontius Pilate" spelled in full
- **PRAEFECTVS IVDAEAE** — the exact title "Prefect of Judaea"

The title is critical. The first-century historian Tacitus (writing ~116 AD) calls Pilate a "procurator" (*procuratorem*) — a different administrative title. The Gospels and Acts do not use a Latin title; they call him the "governor" (Greek: *hegemon*). The Pilate Stone resolves the question definitively: in the 1st century AD, Pilate's actual title was **Prefect** (*praefectus*), an administrative-military rank used for governors of small provinces during the early empire. The title was changed to "Procurator" later in the 1st century, and Tacitus — writing a century after the events — apparently used the then-current title. The Gospels' use of the generic term "governor" is accurate; the stone's "Prefect" is the precise contemporary title.

## Biblical Significance

Before 1961, Pontius Pilate was known from the following sources:
- The four Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John)
- The Acts of the Apostles
- Josephus (*Jewish Antiquities* 18.3.1–2; *Jewish War* 2.9.2–4)
- Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44)
- Philo (*On the Embassy to Gaius* 38)

These literary sources established Pilate's existence with high probability, but no physical artifact or inscription bearing his name had ever been found. The Pilate Stone changed that: for the first time, Pontius Pilate's name appears in stone, in his own province, during his own lifetime, in the standard administrative format of the Roman empire.

The significance for New Testament reliability is substantial:

**1. Confirmation of a named Gospel figure:** The Gospels name Pontius Pilate over a dozen times. He is the central human authority figure in the Passion narrative — the one who formally condemned Jesus to death. The Pilate Stone confirms that this specific individual was indeed the Roman prefect of Judaea at the relevant time, bearing the exact title and operating from the exact location (Caesarea Maritima) that the historical record implies.

**2. Title accuracy:** The stone confirms that the Gospels' use of the generic "governor" (*hegemon*) was appropriate — the actual title was "Prefect," a term Greek-speaking Gospel writers would not necessarily have used precisely, but the stone vindicates their general description.

**3. Caesarea as Pilate's base:** The inscription's discovery at Caesarea Maritima confirms that Caesarea (not Jerusalem) was Pilate's primary administrative seat — consistent with Acts 23–25, which describes later governors operating from Caesarea. Pilate would have come to Jerusalem for major festivals like Passover, which is why he is in the city during the Passion narrative.

**4. The Passion's historical anchor:** Paul's statement in [[2. The Bible (ESV)/54. I Timothy/I Timothy 6|1 Tim 6:13]] — that Jesus "witnessed the good confession before Pontius Pilate" — is among the earliest non-Gospel references to the trial. The stone confirms Pilate's historical reality and role.

## Scholarly Discussion

The authenticity of the Pilate Stone is not questioned — it was found in a controlled archaeological excavation by a credentialed team, in a clearly documented context, and has been studied exhaustively. The debate concerns only the precise reconstruction of the full inscription (particularly the first line), not the key elements: Pilate's name and title are clearly legible.

The stone is sometimes cited in discussions of New Testament historical reliability because it represents the clearest case of an archaeological artifact directly naming and confirming a Gospel figure. Other named NT individuals (Caiaphas, Gallio, Erastus of Corinth) have also been confirmed archaeologically, but the Pilate Stone remains the most prominent example — both because of Pilate's central role in the Passion and because the debate over his historical existence had sometimes been raised in critical scholarship before 1961.

The original stone is in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem, displayed in a case that also shows the reconstruction of the full inscription. A cast replica was installed at Caesarea Maritima at the approximate location of its discovery.
