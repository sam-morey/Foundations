**Type:** Basalt stele inscription (Moabite language, related to Hebrew)
**Date:** ~840 BC
**Discovered:** 1868, near Dhiban (ancient Dibon), Jordan — first identified by Frederick Augustus Klein, an Alsatian missionary; subsequently acquired in fragments
**Current Location:** Louvre Museum, Paris (primary stele); a papier-mâché squeeze made before the stele was broken is held at the Jordan Archaeological Museum, Amman
**Biblical Connection:** 2 Kings 3 (Mesha's revolt); the Omride dynasty; Moab's relationship with Israel
**Key Themes:** [[Judgment]] · [[Sovereignty of God]] · [[Israel & The Gentiles]]

## The Discovery

In 1868, Frederick Augustus Klein, a French-Alsatian missionary traveling in Transjordan, was shown a large black stone by local Bedouin near the ruins of ancient Dibon, biblical Dibon in Moab (modern Dhiban, Jordan). The stone was approximately 1.15 meters tall, 60 cm wide, and covered with 34 lines of inscription in an ancient Northwest Semitic script closely related to Hebrew. Klein recognized its significance and reported the find, setting off a competition between French and German scholars and diplomats to acquire it.

Before the acquisition could be completed, local Bedouin — apparently believing the stone contained treasure — heated it and poured cold water over it, causing it to fracture into fragments. Fortunately, a squeeze (an impression on wet paper or papier-mâché) had already been made, preserving the text of the complete inscription before the fracture. French scholars purchased the surviving fragments, which were reassembled and are now in the Louvre. The squeeze has been used to fill in gaps and reconstruct missing text.

---

## What It Says

The Mesha Stele is a 34-line royal inscription commissioned by King Mesha of Moab, celebrating his political and military victories over Israel. Written in Moabite — a language so closely related to Biblical Hebrew that they are nearly mutually intelligible — the inscription opens:

> "I am Mesha, son of Chemosh-yat, king of Moab, the Dibonite. My father reigned over Moab thirty years, and I reigned after my father."

Key content includes:

- **Reference to "Omri, king of Israel":** "Omri, king of Israel, humbled Moab many years, for Chemosh was angry at his land. And his son followed him and he also said, 'I will humble Moab.'" This directly confirms the biblical Omri dynasty (1 Kings 16:16–28).
- **Mesha's military campaigns:** Moab's reconquest of Israelite territories, including Medeba, Ataroth, Nebo, and Jahaz — all names attested in the Old Testament.
- **The name of YHWH:** The inscription mentions Israel's god: "And the vessels of YHWH, I dragged them before Chemosh." This is a Moabite king using the specific personal name of Israel's God — confirming that YHWH was the distinctive name associated with Israel even from a pagan neighbor's perspective.
- **Probable "House of David" reference (Line 31):** The most contested and exciting recent development: multispectral imaging of the squeeze conducted in 2019 by André Lemaire and Jean-Philippe Delorme revealed traces of letters in line 31 that they reconstruct as **[בי]ת.[דו]ד** (*bytdwd* — "House of David"). If confirmed, this would be the second instance of the Davidic dynasty's name in ancient epigraphy, alongside the Tel Dan Stele. The reading is still debated but increasingly accepted.

## Biblical Significance

The Mesha Stele illuminates one of the more puzzling episodes in Kings. Second Kings 3 records that Mesha, king of Moab, had been paying tribute to Israel (100,000 lambs and wool from 100,000 rams annually, [[2. The Bible (ESV)/12. II Kings/II Kings 3|2 Kings 3:4]]), but after Ahab's death, "the king of Moab rebelled against the king of Israel" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/12. II Kings/II Kings 3|2 Kings 3:5]]). The stele provides Mesha's own account of this rebellion — told from the Moabite perspective, crediting his god Chemosh for the victories. The two accounts — biblical and epigraphic — describe the same historical event from different national and theological vantage points.

The stele is significant on several levels:

**1. The Omri dynasty confirmed:** "Omri, king of Israel" appears by name — the first time a specific Israelite king's name appears in an archaeological inscription (preceding the Tel Dan Stele's reference to the "House of David" by about the same date). Assyrian texts also call Israel "the House of Omri" even generations after Omri's dynasty ended, indicating his dynasty was the one that brought Israel international prominence.

**2. The name YHWH in a foreign inscription:** The Moabite king's reference to "the vessels of YHWH" confirms that YHWH was recognized — by a neighboring people — as the specific name of Israel's national deity. This is not the generic term for "god" (*el*) but the personal divine name that distinguishes Israel's God from all others.

**3. The theological framework:** Mesha attributes his victories to Chemosh's anger and intervention, exactly as Israel's writers attribute events to YHWH's anger and intervention. Both use the same theological grammar — divine punishment, divine redemption, national destiny — applied to their respective deities. This confirms that the biblical writers' way of narrating history was a common ancient Near Eastern genre, not a late theological invention.

**4. The probable "House of David" reference:** If the 2019 reconstruction of line 31 is correct, the Mesha Stele and the Tel Dan Stele together form a double attestation of the Davidic dynasty in enemy royal inscriptions from the 9th century BC.

## Scholarly Discussion

The Mesha Stele has been studied since its discovery and is considered one of the most important inscriptions ever found for biblical history. Debates center on:

- **Line 31 and the "House of David" reading:** Lemaire and Delorme's 2019 multispectral analysis has been accepted by many leading epigraphers (including Christopher Rollston and others who note it is tentative but plausible) and challenged by others. The damaged state of the stele at that point makes absolute certainty impossible, but the reading has gained significant traction.
- **Historical correlations with 2 Kings 3:** The chapter's account of a joint Israelite-Judean-Edomite campaign against Moab that nearly succeeds before Mesha's sacrifice of his son causes "great wrath" to fall on Israel ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/12. II Kings/II Kings 3|2 Kings 3:27]]) is not directly mentioned in the stele (which is from Mesha's perspective, emphasizing his victories). The two accounts are compatible rather than contradictory.
- **Moabite language and biblical connections:** The closeness of Moabite and Hebrew has been used by scholars to demonstrate the shared linguistic and cultural milieu of the Iron Age Levant in which the biblical texts were produced.
