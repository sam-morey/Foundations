## Biblical Events
- ~760 BC — Jonah sent to preach repentance to Nineveh, capital of Assyria; the city repents (Jonah 1–4)
- ~755 BC — Amos prophesies in the northern kingdom against social injustice and idolatry (Amos 1–9)
- ~750–720 BC — Hosea ministers in the northern kingdom; marriage metaphor for Israel's unfaithfulness (Hosea 1–14)
- ~740 BC — Isaiah's vision in the year King Uzziah died; his call to prophetic ministry (Isa 6)
- ~740 BC — Micah prophesies judgment on Samaria and Jerusalem; promises of future restoration (Mic 1–7)
- ~734–732 BC — Tiglath-Pileser III invades Israel; northern territories annexed; Syro-Ephraimite crisis (2 Kgs 15–16; Isa 7)
- ~722 BC — Sargon II captures Samaria; deports the Israelite population; end of the northern kingdom (2 Kgs 17)
- ~715–686 BC — Hezekiah's reign in Judah; religious reforms; tunnel dug to protect Jerusalem's water supply
- ~701 BC — Sennacherib invades Judah; besieges Lachish; 185,000 Assyrians die outside Jerusalem; miraculous deliverance (2 Kgs 18–19; Isa 36–37)
- ~663 BC — Assyria destroys Thebes (No-Amon) in Egypt; mentioned in Nahum as a warning about Nineveh (Nah 3:8)
- ~612 BC — Fall of Nineveh to the Babylonian-Median coalition; the Assyrian Empire collapses (Nah 1–3)

## World History Context

The Neo-Assyrian Empire (c. 911–609 BC) was the most powerful military state the ancient world had yet seen. Beginning with Ashurnasirpal II (r. 883–859 BC), whose palace reliefs at Nimrud (Calah) graphically depict the impalement and flaying of enemies, the Assyrians systematized both warfare and administration on an unprecedented scale. Their innovation of mass deportation — relocating conquered populations to prevent rebellion and replace them with loyal settlers — permanently reshaped the demographics of the ancient Near East and directly produced the "lost ten tribes" of Israel described in 2 Kings 17.

At its greatest extent under Esarhaddon (r. 681–669 BC) and Ashurbanipal (r. 669–627 BC), Assyria controlled territory stretching from Egypt to the Persian Gulf. Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, discovered by Austen Henry Layard in the 1840s, contained over 30,000 cuneiform tablets including the Epic of Gilgamesh (with its famous flood narrative), making it one of the greatest intellectual discoveries of the 19th century. Assyrian art, preserved in the British Museum's Nimrud and Nineveh galleries, includes some of the finest sculpture in the ancient world — as well as detailed battle reliefs recording specific campaigns against Israelite and Judahite cities.

In the wider Mediterranean world, this period saw the end of the Greek Dark Ages and the beginning of the Archaic period: city-states were forming, the Olympic Games were established (~776 BC), Homer's Iliad and Odyssey were written down (c. 750–700 BC), and Greek colonies spread across the Mediterranean from the Black Sea to southern France and Spain. Carthage was founded by Phoenician settlers from Tyre around 814 BC. In Italy, the traditional founding of Rome occurred in 753 BC, although archaeological evidence for an organized settlement on the Tiber dates somewhat later. The convergence of Phoenician, Greek, and emerging Roman civilizations in the west, simultaneously with Assyrian dominance in the east, was forming the cultural world into which the Hebrew prophets would speak.

## Dominant Powers & Their Impact on Israel
| Power | Dates | Impact on Israel/Bible |
|-------|-------|------------------------|
| Neo-Assyrian Empire | 911–609 BC | Destroyed the northern kingdom (722 BC); sieged Jerusalem (701 BC); imposed tribute on Judah; Jonah sent to Nineveh; Nahum predicts Nineveh's fall |
| Egypt (Kushite/25th Dynasty) | 747–664 BC | Provided unreliable military alliance against Assyria that Isaiah warned Hezekiah against (Isa 30–31) |
| Aram-Damascus | 900–732 BC | Northern enemy; allied with Israel against Judah (Syro-Ephraimite war, 734 BC); conquered by Tiglath-Pileser III in 732 BC |
| Babylon (Chaldean) | 626 BC onward | Rose from Assyrian vassalage to destroy Nineveh (612 BC); successor empire that would carry Judah into exile |
| Urartu (Armenia) | 860–590 BC | Northern rival to Assyria; Sargon II's famous 8th campaign against Urartu; the northern frontier of the Assyrian world |

## Key Archaeological Evidence
- **Taylor Prism / Sennacherib's Prism** (c. 691 BC, British Museum) — Six-sided clay prism recording Sennacherib's campaigns; his account of the 701 BC invasion of Judah says he "shut up Hezekiah the Judahite like a bird in a cage" in Jerusalem — notably not claiming to have captured the city, consistent with the biblical account of a miraculous deliverance
- **Lachish Reliefs** (c. 700 BC, British Museum, Room 10b) — Massive sculptural cycle from Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh depicting the siege and capture of Lachish in vivid detail: battering rams, fire arrows, the deportation of captives, and Sennacherib on his throne receiving tribute; one of the most detailed corroborations of a specific biblical battle
- **Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III** (c. 841 BC, British Museum) — Records tribute from "Iaua son of Omri" (King Jehu of Israel); first visual depiction of a named biblical figure
- **Sargon II's Annals and Display Inscriptions** (c. 720–710 BC, Louvre and Oriental Institute, Chicago) — Record the capture of Samaria and deportation of 27,290 Israelites; directly corroborate 2 Kings 17:6
- **Siloam Tunnel and Inscription** (c. 701 BC, Istanbul Archaeological Museum) — Hezekiah's water tunnel cut through 533 meters of solid rock to secure Jerusalem's water supply before Sennacherib's siege; the inscription describes the tunnelers meeting in the middle; corroborates 2 Kings 20:20
- **Ashurbanipal's Library at Nineveh** (7th c. BC, British Museum) — 30,000+ cuneiform tablets including Epic of Gilgamesh (flood narrative), Enuma Elish (creation epic), medical texts, and omen literature; discovered 1847–1851 by Austen Henry Layard and Hormuzd Rassam; illustrates the intellectual world of the empire Nahum declared would fall
- **Nimrud Ivories** (9th–8th c. BC, British Museum and Iraq Museum) — Carved ivory panels looted as tribute from various conquered territories including Israel; show the luxury goods flowing to Assyria that Israel's prophets condemned

## Notable Figures (Biblical & Historical)
| Figure | Role | Significance |
|--------|------|--------------|
| Isaiah | Prophet in Jerusalem | Greatest writing prophet; prophesies both judgment and salvation; chapters 40–55 anticipate the return from exile; chapter 53 depicts the Suffering Servant |
| Amos | Prophet in the northern kingdom | Herdsman from Tekoa; thunders against social injustice and empty religion; "let justice roll down like waters" (Amos 5:24) |
| Hosea | Prophet in the northern kingdom | Marriage to Gomer as enacted parable of Israel's unfaithfulness; profound theology of God's covenant love (hesed) |
| Micah | Prophet in Judah | Predicts Bethlehem as birthplace of the Messiah (Mic 5:2); "do justice, love kindness, walk humbly" (Mic 6:8) |
| Hezekiah | King of Judah | Religious reformer; destroyed high places; trusted God during Sennacherib's siege; prayed and Jerusalem was miraculously spared |
| Tiglath-Pileser III | King of Assyria | Reorganized Assyrian military and deportation policies; first Assyrian king to annexe Israelite territory (734 BC) |
| Sargon II | King of Assyria | Completed capture of Samaria (722 BC); deported Israelite population; settled foreign peoples in the land |
| Sennacherib | King of Assyria | Invaded Judah (701 BC); captured 46 cities; besieged Jerusalem; 185,000 troops die miraculously; later assassinated by his sons |
| Ashurbanipal | King of Assyria | Last great Assyrian king; builder of Nineveh's library; his death ~627 BC triggered the empire's rapid collapse |
