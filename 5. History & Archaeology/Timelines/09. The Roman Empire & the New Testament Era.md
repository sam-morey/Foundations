# The Roman Empire & the New Testament Era — Historical Context

## Biblical Events
- ~63 BC — Pompey takes Jerusalem; Judea becomes a Roman client state; end of Hasmonean independence
- ~37–4 BC — Reign of Herod the Great; massive building program including the renovation of the Second Temple; massacre of the innocents
- ~6–5 BC — Birth of Jesus in Bethlehem of Judea, during the reign of Herod the Great (Matt 2; Luke 2)
- ~4 BC — Herod the Great dies; his kingdom divided among his sons (Archelaus, Antipas, Philip)
- ~6 AD — Judea becomes a direct Roman province under a prefect; Quirinius census triggers the Zealot movement (Acts 5:37)
- ~26–36 AD — Pontius Pilate serves as prefect of Judea under Emperor Tiberius
- ~27–30 AD — Ministry of Jesus: baptism by John in the Jordan, temptation, calling of disciples, Sermon on the Mount, miracles, transfiguration
- ~30 AD — Crucifixion of Jesus under Pontius Pilate; burial; resurrection; post-resurrection appearances (all four Gospels)
- ~30 AD — Pentecost; the Holy Spirit descends; the church born in Jerusalem (Acts 2)
- ~32–35 AD — Conversion of Saul of Tarsus on the Damascus road (Acts 9)
- ~46–48 AD — Paul's first missionary journey (Acts 13–14); churches planted in Galatia
- ~50–52 AD — Paul in Corinth; Gallio as proconsul of Achaia (Acts 18); earliest NT letters (1–2 Thessalonians) written
- ~60–62 AD — Paul imprisoned in Caesarea, then Rome; Prison Epistles written (Philippians, Colossians, Ephesians, Philemon)
- ~64 AD — Great Fire of Rome; Nero blames Christians; first Roman imperial persecution; deaths of Peter and Paul in Rome
- ~66–70 AD — Jewish-Roman War; siege of Jerusalem; Temple destroyed by Titus (70 AD); Josephus records the events
- ~79 AD — Eruption of Vesuvius buries Pompeii and Herculaneum; froze a snapshot of Roman life at the time of the early church
- ~81–96 AD — Reign of Domitian; second major Roman persecution of Christians; Revelation likely written (~95 AD)
- ~100 AD — Death of the Apostle John in Ephesus; close of the apostolic age

## World History Context

The Roman Republic gave way to the Empire when Octavian (Augustus Caesar) defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra at the Battle of Actium (31 BC) and became the first Roman emperor in 27 BC. Augustus's reign (27 BC–14 AD) inaugurated the Pax Romana — a 200-year period of relative peace, unprecedented infrastructure development, and economic integration across the Mediterranean world. Roman roads, some 250,000 miles of paved military highways, connected every corner of the empire from Britain to Mesopotamia. The Roman postal system, sea lanes kept free of pirates after Pompey's campaigns, and the universal use of Greek (koine) in the eastern empire and Latin in the west created the most favorable conditions for communication the ancient world had ever known. The Apostle Paul exploited this infrastructure systematically in his missionary journeys.

It was precisely "when the fullness of time had come" (Gal 4:4) — into this uniquely connected, linguistically unified, administratively organized world — that God sent his Son. The Roman census system (Luke 2:1) brought Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem; Roman roads carried the gospel to Rome within 30 years; Roman citizenship protected Paul (Acts 22:25–29) and gave him access to the imperial court; Roman legal proceedings preserved Paul's letters as he wrote from prison. The empire that crucified Jesus became, paradoxically, the infrastructure of Christian expansion.

The Julio-Claudian dynasty (Augustus, Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius, Nero) spans the entire period of the Gospels and Acts. Tiberius (r. 14–37 AD) was emperor during Jesus's ministry and crucifixion. Claudius (r. 41–54 AD) expelled the Jews from Rome around 49 AD — referenced in Acts 18:2 when Aquila and Priscilla arrive in Corinth, having "recently come from Italy." Nero's persecution (64–68 AD) killed Peter and Paul and is almost certainly alluded to in 1 Peter and Revelation. The Jewish-Roman War (66–70 AD) fulfilled Jesus's explicit prophecy about the Temple's destruction (Matt 24:2; Luke 21:6), with Titus's army burning the Temple and removing its treasures — depicted on the Arch of Titus in Rome, which still stands. The Flavian dynasty (Vespasian, Titus, Domitian) brought further persecution under Domitian (r. 81–96 AD), the most likely context for the book of Revelation with its coded language about Rome as "Babylon" and its call to faithful endurance.

| Power | Dates | Impact on Israel/Bible |

| Roman Republic/Empire | 63 BC–100 AD | Occupied and governed Judea; crucified Jesus; provided infrastructure for the gospel's spread; persecuted the early church |
| Herodian Dynasty | 37 BC–92 AD | Client kings under Rome; Herod the Great built the Temple; Herod Antipas executed John the Baptist; Agrippa I executed James |
| Nabataean Kingdom | 4th c. BC–106 AD | Arab kingdom to the east; Paul fled to Arabia (Gal 1:17); Aretas IV's ethnarch tried to arrest Paul in Damascus (2 Cor 11:32) |
| Parthian Empire | 247 BC–224 AD | Eastern rival to Rome; Jewish diaspora communities in Parthia represented at Pentecost (Acts 2:9); Revelation's "kings from the east" may allude to Parthia |

## Key Archaeological Evidence
- **Pilate Stone** (c. 26–36 AD, discovered 1961 at Caesarea Maritima, now Israel Museum) — Limestone inscription bearing the Latin text: "Pontius Pilatus, Prefect of Judea" — the only direct archaeological confirmation of Pontius Pilate's historicity and title (the NT calls him governor; the inscription uses the earlier title "prefect," exactly as Tacitus does)
- **Ossuary of Caiaphas** (c. 1st c. AD, discovered 1990 in Jerusalem's Peace Forest, Israel Museum) — Elaborately carved limestone bone-box inscribed twice with "Joseph son of Caiaphas"; contained the bones of an elderly male; almost certainly the ossuary of the high priest who condemned Jesus (Matt 26:57; John 18:13–14)
- **Gallio Inscription at Delphi** (c. 52 AD, Delphi Museum and scattered) — Fragment of an imperial letter from Claudius mentioning Gallio as proconsul of Achaia; enables scholars to date Paul's 18-month stay in Corinth to approximately 50–52 AD (Acts 18:12–17), providing one of the most secure chronological anchors in NT history
- **Nazareth Inscription** (c. 1st c. AD, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris) — Greek marble slab bearing an imperial edict (possibly Claudius or Tiberius) declaring capital punishment for anyone who "destroys, damages, or removes" a burial; possibly a response to reports reaching Rome about claims that Jesus's tomb was found empty
- **Arch of Titus** (82 AD, Roman Forum) — Relief panels depicting the triumphal procession after Jerusalem's fall (70 AD); includes the famous scene of Roman soldiers carrying the Temple's seven-branched menorah — visual corroboration of the Temple's destruction described in Josephus and predicted by Jesus
- **Ossuary of James** ("James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus"; debated authenticity, c. 1st c. AD) — Aramaic inscription; provenance disputed and authenticity contested, but if genuine would be the earliest reference to Jesus outside the NT; case ongoing in scholarly debate
- **Pompeii and Herculaneum** (buried 79 AD, excavated since 1748) — Freeze-frame of Roman provincial life exactly at the time of the early church; graffiti, shops, homes, synagogue remains (at Ostia, Rome's port), and early Christian symbols (possible cross graffiti at Herculaneum) illuminate the daily world of NT Christians
- **Claudius's Edict Expelling Jews from Rome** (~49 AD, referenced by Suetonius in "Lives of the Twelve Caesars") — Suetonius notes Claudius expelled Jews "at the instigation of Chrestus" (likely a reference to disputes about Christ); directly explains Aquila and Priscilla's presence in Corinth (Acts 18:2)
- **Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews** (93–94 AD) — Jewish historian Flavius Josephus mentions Jesus (the Testimonium Flavianum, 18.63–64; even in its disputed form it confirms Jesus's existence, crucifixion under Pilate, and the resurrection faith of his followers), James "the brother of Jesus called Christ" (20.200), and John the Baptist (18.116–119)

## Notable Figures (Biblical & Historical)
| Figure | Role | Significance |
|--------|------|--------------|
| Jesus of Nazareth | Son of God; Messiah; Lord | The central figure of all human history; fulfillment of the entire OT; crucified under Pilate; raised on the third day |
| Augustus Caesar | First Roman Emperor | "A decree went out from Caesar Augustus" (Luke 2:1); his census brought Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem |
| Herod the Great | King of Judea (client of Rome) | Renovated the Temple on a grand scale; tried to kill the infant Jesus; his death ~4 BC places Jesus's birth before it |
| John the Baptist | Prophet; forerunner of Jesus | Fulfills the Elijah role (Mal 4:5; Matt 11:14); "Behold, the Lamb of God" (John 1:29); executed by Herod Antipas |
| Pontius Pilate | Roman prefect of Judea | Condemned Jesus to death; inscription at Caesarea Maritima confirms his historicity and title |
| Caiaphas | High priest | Presided over Jesus's trial; declared it expedient that one man die for the people (John 11:50); ossuary discovered 1990 |
| Paul (Saul of Tarsus) | Apostle to the Gentiles | Roman citizen from Tarsus; wrote 13 NT letters; three missionary journeys from Antioch to Rome; martyred under Nero |
| Peter (Simon bar Jonah) | Apostle; first church leader | Preached at Pentecost; leadership of Jerusalem church; wrote 1–2 Peter; martyred (crucified upside down) under Nero |
| Titus | Roman general; Emperor | Destroyed Jerusalem and the Temple (70 AD); his triumph depicted on the Arch of Titus in Rome |
| Josephus (Flavius) | Jewish historian | Eyewitness to the Jewish-Roman War; wrote Antiquities and Jewish War; mentions Jesus, John the Baptist, and James |
