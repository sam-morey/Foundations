# Stephen

**Testament:** New Testament
**Era:** The Early Church
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/44. Acts Overview|Acts]]

## Who He Is
Stephen is the first Christian martyr — one of the seven men chosen by the Jerusalem church to oversee the distribution of food to widows, who quickly became a powerful preacher and worker of signs. When dragged before the Sanhedrin on false charges, he delivered the longest sermon in the book of Acts: a sweeping retelling of Israel's history from Abraham to Solomon, culminating in the charge that his accusers were repeating the pattern of every generation that rejected God's messengers. He died stoned, praying for his murderers — with a young man named Saul watching and approving.

## Why He Matters
- His speech in Acts 7 is one of the most theologically dense passages in Acts — it argues that the Temple and the law were always provisional, pointing toward Jesus, and that Israel's history is a repeated pattern of rejecting the very messengers God sent
- His death mirrors Jesus's death in specific ways: false witnesses, prayer for his murderers, committing his spirit to God — showing that following Jesus includes following him in the pattern of his death
- His martyrdom scatters the Jerusalem church (Acts 8:1), which then spreads the gospel to Judea, Samaria, and beyond — the very mission Jesus had commissioned in Acts 1:8

## Character & Legacy
Stephen was a man whose inner life matched his outer courage. To stand before the Sanhedrin — the body that had condemned Jesus — and accuse them of being stiff-necked, uncircumcised in heart, resisters of the Holy Spirit (Acts 7:51) is not political savvy; it is the kind of Spirit-filled fearlessness that had no calculation for self-preservation. He was not trying to survive the hearing.

Saul of Tarsus watched Stephen die, holding the cloaks of the men who threw the stones (Acts 7:58). Luke mentions this detail twice (Acts 7:58; 8:1), and it is hard not to read the later conversion of Paul against the backdrop of Stephen's prayer: "Lord, do not hold this sin against them." The first martyr's intercession may have been part of the answer God used to call the church's greatest apostle.
