## Who They Are
Solomon is the son of David and Bathsheba, the third king of Israel and the builder of the Jerusalem Temple. God gives him wisdom beyond any man before or after him, and in the early years of his reign he exemplifies what godly kingship can look like. Yet his seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines — many from forbidden nations — turn his heart away from God in his old age, and it is his idolatry that plants the seed of the kingdom's division. He is the author of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song of Solomon.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Solomon's story is a sobering demonstration that God-given gifts — wisdom, wealth, influence — can become instruments of idolatry when not held in submission to God. Wisdom applied apart from the fear of God becomes folly.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Solomon builds the Temple that gives Israel a permanent center of worship and anticipates the presence of God dwelling with his people fully in Christ. His writings form the core of Israel's wisdom tradition, shaping how God's people think about life, work, love, and meaning.
- **Legacy:** Solomon is the hinge point of Israel's history — the height of the United Monarchy and the beginning of its fracture. His fall is not a surprise ending but the warning Deuteronomy 17 saw coming: kings who multiply wives and wealth and horses will have their hearts turned away.

## Character & Legacy
Solomon's early request for wisdom is genuinely admirable — in the face of an open invitation from God, he asks not for himself but for the capacity to serve his people well. And God honors that. The Temple he builds is extraordinary; his dedication prayer is one of the most theologically profound texts in the Old Testament. He understands that God cannot be contained in a building, yet asks that the building might be a place where prayer is heard.

But the seeds of failure were there from early on — he married Pharaoh's daughter even in his early reign. The trajectory becomes unmistakable: the foreign wives bring their foreign gods, and Solomon builds high places for them. The man who asked for a hearing heart ends with a divided heart. His death leaves a ruptured kingdom. The book of Ecclesiastes may be read as the memoir of a man who pursued everything wisdom, pleasure, and power could offer and found it all vapor — but who ends by returning to the fear of God as the only thing that matters.
