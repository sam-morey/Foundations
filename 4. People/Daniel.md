## Who They Are
Daniel is a young Jewish nobleman taken to Babylon in the first deportation, who rises to serve in Nebuchadnezzar's court while maintaining faithful witness to the God of Israel. He interprets dreams, survives the lion's den, and receives the most sweeping apocalyptic visions in the Old Testament — including the vision of the Son of Man coming with the clouds of heaven (Daniel 7), which Jesus uses as his primary self-description. He is the model of a believer living faithfully as an exile in a pagan empire.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Daniel demonstrates that God's sovereignty is total — over pagan kings, over the fiery furnace, over the lion's mouth, and over the course of history. His faithfulness is grounded not in certainty of physical deliverance but in certainty of God's character: "our God is able… but even if he does not."
- **Role in redemptive history:** Daniel 7's vision of the Son of Man is the key title Jesus takes for himself in the Gospels and before the Sanhedrin. Daniel's visions of the four kingdoms and the everlasting kingdom are the scaffolding for understanding Revelation and the consummation of history.
- **Legacy:** Daniel is the premier model of faithful witness in a hostile, pluralistic culture — maintaining conviction without withdrawing from engagement, serving with excellence while refusing to worship what cannot save.

## Character & Legacy
Daniel's character is defined by an unbroken practice of prayer and an unwillingness to be assimilated. He serves Babylon at the highest level but his identity is never Babylonian. When the law is passed to prevent prayer to anyone but the king, he does not stop — he prays at his open window as he had always done. There is no calculation, no heroic gesture; he simply continues his practice. Faithfulness formed over years does not require a crisis-by-crisis decision.

His apocalyptic visions disturb him deeply — he grows pale, loses strength, lies sick for days after seeing them. He is not a detached seer; he participates in the weight of what he sees. His prayer of confession in Daniel 9 is one of the great intercessory prayers of Scripture — he identifies with the sin of his people ("we have sinned") though he is personally the most righteous character in the book. He stands in the gap for his generation.
