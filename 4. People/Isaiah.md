## Who They Are
Isaiah son of Amoz is the greatest of the Old Testament writing prophets, whose ministry spans four Judean kings from Uzziah to Hezekiah. Called the "fifth evangelist," his book contains the most explicit anticipations of the Messiah in all of Scripture, including the Servant Songs (Isaiah 42, 49, 50, 52–53) that describe the crucifixion in detail seven centuries before it happens. His vision of God enthroned in the Temple (Isaiah 6) is the definitive OT call narrative, and his message of comfort in Isaiah 40–66 is quoted more in the New Testament than any other prophet.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Isaiah sees the holiness of God more clearly than any other prophet — his throne-room vision establishes the absolute purity of God and the corresponding uncleanness of humanity. From this foundation flows everything else: judgment is just, grace is astonishing, and the coming salvation will be entirely God's work.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Isaiah 53 is the single most important Old Testament text for understanding the atonement. The New Testament writers quote it repeatedly to explain what the cross accomplished. Without Isaiah, the theology of substitutionary atonement would lose its deepest biblical roots.
- **Legacy:** Isaiah's influence on the New Testament is unparalleled. He is quoted over 400 times in the NT. He gave the church its language for the incarnation ("Immanuel"), the forerunner ("A voice crying in the wilderness"), the mission of Jesus (Isaiah 61), and the new creation (Isaiah 65–66).

## Character & Legacy
Isaiah's response to his throne-room vision is the most honest response to God's holiness in Scripture: "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips." He does not dress up his condition. The coal from the altar — a burning, painful purification — is the answer. He is not cleaned by his own effort but by God's initiative. And immediately he volunteers: "Here I am. Send me." The call is not preceded by credentials but by confession and cleansing.

His ministry is one of the most difficult imaginable. God tells him at his call that his message will harden hearts rather than soften them — he is to preach to a people who will not listen, until the land lies desolate. He does it anyway, for decades. His later chapters (40–66) bloom into some of the most magnificent poetry in all of human literature: the comfort of the exiles, the servant who bears our griefs, the invitation to the thirsty, the promise of the new heavens and new earth.
