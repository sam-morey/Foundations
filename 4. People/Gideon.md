# Gideon

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** The Judges
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/07. Judges Overview|Judges]]

## Who They Are
Gideon is a judge in Israel who delivers the nation from the oppression of the Midianites through a miraculous victory with a force of only 300 men, armed with trumpets and torches rather than conventional weapons. He is called while hiding from the enemy, introduces himself as the least in his clan, and repeatedly asks God for confirming signs. His story is a powerful illustration of God's strength displayed through human weakness — and a sobering portrait of how a man who begins with radical dependence on God can end in tragic spiritual failure.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** The reduction of Gideon's army from 32,000 to 300 is God's explicit strategy: "lest Israel boast over me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me'" (Judges 7:2). The victory is structured so that no human being can claim credit. It is a parable of the gospel — salvation is God's achievement, not ours.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Gideon's story is the fullest expression in Judges of the principle that God delivers through weakness. Paul picks up this same logic in 1 Corinthians 1: God chose the foolish things to shame the wise, the weak to shame the strong. The 300 men with torches and trumpets are an Old Testament image of that same gospel pattern.
- **Legacy:** Gideon is remembered in Hebrews 11 for his faith, but his story in Judges ends in tragedy and serves as a warning. His golden ephod becomes an idol. His son Abimelech becomes the first attempted tyrant-king of Israel. The cycle of Judges — deliverance, faithfulness, apostasy, oppression — runs right through Gideon's own household.

## Character & Legacy
Gideon begins at his lowest: threshing wheat in a winepress, hidden from the Midianites. When the angel calls him a "mighty warrior," it is entirely ironic — he is hiding. His immediate self-assessment ("my clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house") is not false modesty but accurate description. And this is exactly whom God calls. His asking for signs — the fleece, first dry and then wet — is typically read as weak faith, but God graciously accommodates it. Gideon's campaign is animated by radical trust in a God who keeps reducing the odds.

But success undoes what adversity built. After the victory, Gideon takes the gold earrings from the plunder and makes an ephod — a priestly garment or religious object — that "all Israel whored after." He fathers seventy sons by many wives, and one concubine's son (Abimelech) will slaughter sixty-nine of his brothers on a stone. Gideon's own refusal of kingship ("the Lord will rule over you") sounds principled, but his behavior was already functionally royal. He is a man who understood grace in the darkest hour and forgot it in the brightest. This pattern — faith under pressure, drift in prosperity — is one of Judges' central warnings.
