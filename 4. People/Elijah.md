## Who They Are
Elijah is the great prophet of the Northern Kingdom who confronts Baal worship during the reign of Ahab and Jezebel, calling Israel back to the exclusive worship of the LORD. He is fed by ravens in the wilderness, raises a widow's son, defeats the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel, and hears God not in wind or earthquake or fire but in a still, small voice. He is taken to heaven in a whirlwind without dying and is expected to return before the Day of the Lord — a prophecy fulfilled in John the Baptist.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Elijah demonstrates that one faithful person, standing on God's word, can confront an entire culture's idolatry. His ministry is a sustained argument that the LORD — not Baal — is the living God who controls rain, fire, life, and death.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Elijah's expected return before the Day of the Lord (Malachi 4:5) is one of the most important OT prophecies, fulfilled in John the Baptist's ministry of preparation for the Messiah. Jesus confirms this explicitly.
- **Legacy:** Elijah stands with Moses on the Mount of Transfiguration, representing the Law and the Prophets bearing witness to Jesus. He is one of only two people in the Bible taken to heaven without dying, and his story ends in expectation — he is to come again.

## Character & Legacy
Elijah is a man of enormous courage and alarming fragility — often in the same chapter. He stands alone against 450 prophets on Mount Carmel with complete calm. Then, when Jezebel threatens his life, he runs to the wilderness and prays to die. This is not hypocrisy; it is the authentic portrait of a human being who has poured out everything for God and is empty. God's response to his exhaustion is not rebuke but bread and water, sleep and rest — followed by a gentle question: "What are you doing here, Elijah?"

The still, small voice on Horeb is one of the most important moments in all of Scripture. God is not found in the dramatic spectacles — the wind, the earthquake, the fire — but in the gentle, almost imperceptible voice. Elijah learns that the battle is not about spectacle; the 7,000 who have not bowed to Baal never made the news. Faithfulness is often hidden.

## New Testament Connections
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/40. Matthew/Matthew 11|Matthew 11:13–14]] — Jesus explicitly identifies John the Baptist as "the Elijah who was to come"
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/40. Matthew/Matthew 17|Matthew 17:1–5]] — Elijah appears with Moses at the Transfiguration, flanking Jesus and representing the Law and the Prophets
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/42. Luke/Luke 4|Luke 4:25–26]] — Jesus cites Elijah's ministry to the widow of Zarephath as an example of grace going to Gentiles
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/45. Romans/Romans 11|Romans 11:2–4]] — Paul quotes the Elijah narrative about the remnant of 7,000 to argue that God has not rejected his people
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/59. James/James 5|James 5:17–18]] — Elijah cited as a model of fervent prayer: "a man with a nature like ours" who prayed and changed the weather
