# Moses

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** The Exodus
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/02. Exodus Overview|Exodus]], [[3. Overviews/03. Leviticus Overview|Leviticus]], [[3. Overviews/04. Numbers Overview|Numbers]], [[3. Overviews/05. Deuteronomy Overview|Deuteronomy]]

## Who They Are
Moses is the paradigmatic prophet, deliverer, and lawgiver of the Old Testament — the man through whom God redeemed Israel out of Egypt, gave the Torah at Sinai, and mediated the covenant that defined Israel as a nation. No figure in the Hebrew Bible looms larger. He speaks face to face with God, leads an entire nation through the wilderness for forty years, and at his death God himself buries him. Yet Deuteronomy 18 and John 5 both insist that Moses points beyond himself to a greater prophet still to come.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Moses reveals the character of God as both holy and merciful — the God who will not tolerate sin but who meets his people with compassion and redemption. The Law given through Moses is not a means of salvation but a revelation of God's character and humanity's need for a Mediator.
- **Role in redemptive history:** The Exodus that Moses leads is the defining act of salvation in the Old Testament, the event Israel's entire worship calendar commemorated. Every subsequent redemption in Scripture echoes its pattern, and Jesus's own death and resurrection is presented in the Gospels as the new and greater Exodus.
- **Legacy:** Moses is the lens through which Jesus is understood by the New Testament writers. John opens his Gospel comparing Moses and Jesus. Jesus gives the Sermon on the Mount as the new Moses. Peter and James and John see Moses and Elijah flanking Jesus at the Transfiguration.

## Character & Legacy
Moses is one of the most fully human figures in the Bible. He is reluctant (four objections at the burning bush), prone to anger (he murders an Egyptian, smashes the tablets, strikes the rock), intercedes passionately for Israel when God proposes to destroy them, and admits his own inadequacy repeatedly. Yet he is also described as "very meek, more than all people who were on the face of the earth" (Numbers 12:3) — a man shaped by the weight of his calling rather than inflated by it.

His exclusion from the Promised Land for striking the rock in anger is one of the most sobering moments in the Torah. After forty years of faithfulness, one act of unauthorized anger disqualifies him. This is not cruelty — it is the text's insistence that those who represent God to the people bear a higher accountability. And yet Moses dies having seen the land, buried by God himself, and appearing on the Mount of Transfiguration with Jesus — present in the land he was denied in life. Grace outlasts even discipline.
