# Joshua

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** The Conquest
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/05. Deuteronomy Overview|Deuteronomy]], [[3. Overviews/06. Joshua Overview|Joshua]]

## Who They Are
Joshua is the military commander and covenant leader who succeeds Moses and leads the generation of Israelites born in the wilderness into the Promised Land. His name — Yeshua in Hebrew — is the same name as Jesus, and the New Testament letter to the Hebrews draws explicit attention to the significance of this: the rest that Joshua gave was real, but incomplete. The true rest that God's people seek is only found in Jesus. Joshua is the paradigmatic faithful warrior who trusts God's word even when it demands impossible courage.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Joshua demonstrates that faith is not passive waiting but active obedience — marching around Jericho, stepping into the Jordan, trusting God's battle plans over military convention. His life is a case study in what it looks like to be "strong and courageous" because God is present.
- **Role in redemptive history:** The conquest of Canaan is the fulfillment of the promise made to Abraham — land for his descendants. Joshua's leadership represents the moment the long journey from Ur finally arrives. But Hebrews insists the deeper geography — rest in God — was only pointed to, not achieved, by the conquest.
- **Legacy:** Joshua's name, identical to Jesus, is not accidental in New Testament interpretation. He is the leader named Yeshua who brings God's people to the land; Jesus is the leader named Yeshua who brings God's people to the true rest. Hebrews 4 makes the typological argument explicit.

## Character & Legacy
Joshua is remarkable for his long apprenticeship and his consistent obedience. He first appears as a commander against the Amalekites, then as Moses's attendant at the tent of meeting, then as one of twelve spies — and is one of only two who give a faithful report. He serves for decades in the shadow of Moses without apparent resentment or ambition, and when the call finally comes, he is ready.

His greatest failure is not military but diplomatic: he is deceived by the Gibeonites (Joshua 9) who appear as distant travelers and secure a treaty under false pretenses. Joshua and the elders make the covenant without seeking God's counsel. This oath later creates complications but is honored anyway — a reminder that covenant fidelity matters even when it is costly. His covenant renewal speech at Shechem (Joshua 24) is one of the most compelling calls to decision in the Old Testament: he does not command, he chooses. "As for me and my house, we will serve the Lord." Leadership by example, not compulsion.
