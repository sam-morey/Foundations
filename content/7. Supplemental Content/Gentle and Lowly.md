## Synopsis
- Dane Ortlund argues that the deepest truth about Jesus is not his power or his teachings but his heart — gentle and lowly toward sinners and sufferers.
- Drawing on 23 biblical passages, he shows that Christ's affections for his people are not reluctant or measured but superabundant and freely given.
- Understanding the heart of Christ is the foundation of a durable, joy-filled Christian life rather than a life of anxious striving.

## Source Info
- Title: Gentle and Lowly: The Heart of Christ for Sinners and Sufferers
- Author: Dane Ortlund
- Publication Date: 2020
- Themes: Grace & Mercy, Love, Messiah & Christ, Faith & Trust

## Key Ideas
- Matthew 11:29 — "I am gentle and lowly in heart" — is the only place in the Gospels where Jesus describes his own heart, and it deserves extended meditation.
- The Old Testament "bowels" language (splanchna in Greek) communicates visceral, gut-level compassion that cannot be subdued — Christ's mercy is not a policy but a passion.
- The High Priest imagery in Hebrews shows that Jesus sympathizes with weakness from the inside, not as an observer, and that his intercession never ceases.
- The doctrine of divine impassibility does not mean God is emotionally cold; rather, the incarnate Son entered into genuine human affections to save and to sympathize.

## Chapter Summaries
- **Chapter 1 — His Heart**
  - **Main Idea:** Matthew 11:29 is the single verse where Jesus reveals his own inner life, and the claim he makes is that his heart is gentle and lowly.
  - **Key Points:**
    - "Gentle" (praus) carries the sense of not being easily angered or provoked — a disposition of non-retaliation.
    - "Lowly" (tapeinos) in a first-century context meant humble, unpretentious, close to the ground.
    - Jesus is not merely describing a behavioral pattern but disclosing his inner orientation toward those who come to him.
    - The invitation ("come to me, all who labor") is inseparable from the disclosure of his heart — the heart is the basis for the invitation.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Praus:** Greek word for meekness or gentleness; used of a tamed horse — power under control, not weakness.
    - **Tapeinos:** Greek word for lowly or humble; denotes lack of pretension or self-assertion.
  - **Takeaway:** Christ's heart toward those who come to him in need is the most reliable data point for understanding who he is.

- **Chapter 2 — Bowels of Mercy**
  - **Main Idea:** The Old Testament and New Testament both use visceral "bowels" language to describe God's compassion, communicating that his mercy is gut-level and irrepressible.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Hebrew rahamim (tender mercies) derives from the word for womb — the most intimate, protective love imaginable.
    - Greek splanchna (bowels, inward parts) is the word used for Jesus being "moved with compassion" in the Gospels.
    - This language was chosen precisely because it cannot be rationalized away — it points to something deeper than policy or duty.
    - The father in the parable of the prodigal son is said to have splanchna when he sees his son returning — Jesus is painting a picture of God's own heart.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Rahamim:** Hebrew plural noun for tender mercies or compassion; etymologically connected to the word for womb (rechem).
    - **Splanchna:** Greek noun for the inward organs; used metaphorically for deep, visceral compassion in both biblical and extra-biblical literature.
  - **Takeaway:** God's compassion in Scripture is not polite sympathy but a gut-wrenching, womb-like tenderness that defines who he is.

- **Chapter 3 — The Emotional Life of Christ**
  - **Main Idea:** The Gospels portray Jesus with a genuine emotional life — not manufactured for effect but authentically human and revealing of the divine heart.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Jesus wept at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35) even though he knew he was about to raise him — the grief was real, not performative.
    - Jesus felt compassion for the crowds because they were "harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd" (Matthew 9:36).
    - Jesus expressed distress, sorrow, and even a kind of holy anger throughout the Gospels.
    - Theologians have sometimes minimized Christ's emotions out of a concern for divine impassibility, but this reading impoverishes our understanding of the incarnation.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Divine Impassibility:** The classical theological claim that God does not suffer or change emotionally in the way creatures do; rightly understood, it does not deny that the incarnate Son had genuine affections.
    - **Incarnation:** The eternal Son of God taking on human flesh and nature in the person of Jesus of Nazareth.
  - **Takeaway:** Christ's emotional life in the Gospels is not an embarrassment to be explained away but a window into the compassionate heart of God.

- **Chapter 4 — A Tender Friend**
  - **Main Idea:** Hebrews 2 presents Jesus as the one who is not ashamed to call his people brothers and sisters, locating him on the side of the weak rather than the strong.
  - **Key Points:**
    - "He is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Hebrews 2:11) — the language of shame reversal is significant in an honor-shame culture.
    - Jesus entered fully into the human condition — suffering, temptation, death — so that he could be a fully sympathetic helper.
    - The word "helper" (boethos) in Hebrews 2:18 has a military connotation of running to the aid of someone under attack.
    - Christ's solidarity with the weak is not a temporary posture adopted during the incarnation but a permanent feature of his priestly identity.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Boethos:** Greek word used in Hebrews 2:18 meaning helper or one who runs to assist; has a battlefield resonance of coming to the aid of the fallen.
  - **Takeaway:** Jesus is the kind of friend who runs toward you in your weakness rather than distancing himself from your mess.

- **Chapter 5 — What Our Mediator Is Like**
  - **Main Idea:** Hebrews 4:14–16 presents the high priest imagery to show that our mediator before God is one who has been tested as we are tested and who therefore invites rather than repels our approach.
  - **Key Points:**
    - "We do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses" — the double negative is emphatic: he absolutely can sympathize.
    - "Tempted as we are, yet without sin" — the sinlessness does not create distance but rather means his sympathy is unclouded by his own failures.
    - The throne of grace (Hebrews 4:16) is only a throne of grace because the one seated at it is gentle and lowly.
    - The call to "draw near with confidence" flows directly from the nature of the mediator, not from our own performance.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **High Priest:** In the Old Testament, the one mediator between Israel and God who entered the Holy of Holies; in Hebrews, this role is fulfilled and surpassed by Jesus.
    - **Sympathy:** From Greek sympatheo — to suffer with, to enter into the experience of another from the inside.
  - **Takeaway:** Our confidence in prayer rests not on our worthiness but on the character of the one to whom we pray.

- **Chapter 6 — The Happiness of God**
  - **Main Idea:** God is not a reluctant forgiver; he takes delight in showing mercy, and the Psalms and Prophets repeatedly portray him as a God who is happy to be gracious.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Micah 7:18 asks "Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity?" and answers that he delights in steadfast love — mercy is not a concession but a joy.
    - The father in Luke 15 does not merely allow the son to return; he runs, embraces, and throws a party — the excess signals divine exuberance.
    - A God who is grudging in forgiveness would not have sent his Son; the cross is the supreme demonstration that God is wholehearted in his saving work.
    - When we approach God expecting reluctance, we have believed a lie about his character.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Steadfast Love (Hesed):** Hebrew covenant word for loyal, faithful, enduring love; it carries connotations of commitment and delight, not mere duty.
  - **Takeaway:** God does not forgive us against his nature but according to it — mercy is not foreign to him but central.

- **Chapter 7 — His Heart in Action: Calming the Storm**
  - **Main Idea:** The miracles of Jesus in the Gospels are not merely demonstrations of power but windows into his heart — he acts out of compassion, not obligation.
  - **Key Points:**
    - In Mark 4, Jesus calms the storm and then asks "Why are you so afraid?" — the rebuke is gentle, not harsh.
    - The disciples' fear reveals their misunderstanding of who is in the boat with them.
    - Jesus is not annoyed by the disciples' panic; his question is an invitation to reorient their trust.
    - The miracle demonstrates both his power and his patience with slow-faith disciples.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Theophany:** A manifestation or appearance of God; the nature miracles of Jesus carry theophanic overtones, echoing Old Testament acts of divine power over creation.
  - **Takeaway:** The same Jesus who calmed the storm is present in every storm his people face, and his disposition toward the panicking disciples is telling.

- **Chapter 8 — His Heart in Action: Healing the Sick**
  - **Main Idea:** The healing miracles of Jesus consistently begin with compassion, not with a test of worthiness, revealing that his power is harnessed to his mercy.
  - **Key Points:**
    - When the leper says "if you are willing, you can make me clean," Jesus replies "I am willing" (Mark 1:40–41) — the willingness is the first word.
    - Jesus touches the leper before healing him — the touch itself was an act of mercy in a culture where lepers were untouchable.
    - The blind man Bartimaeus is told to be quiet by the crowd, but Jesus stops and heals him — social pressure does not diminish his attention to individual need.
    - The healings are previews of the new creation, but they are also personal acts of compassion toward specific, named sufferers.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Ceremonial Uncleanness:** In Mosaic law, a state that required separation from the community and from worship; lepers were among the most socially excluded on these grounds.
  - **Takeaway:** Jesus's healings reveal a pattern: wherever there is need and a cry for mercy, his heart moves toward rather than away.

- **Chapter 9 — His Work Is to Intercede**
  - **Main Idea:** The ongoing, present-tense intercession of Christ at the right hand of the Father (Hebrews 7:25; Romans 8:34) means that his compassion is not past-tense but continually active.
  - **Key Points:**
    - "He always lives to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25) — the word "always" is absolute; there is no moment when he is not interceding.
    - Romans 8:34 places the intercession of Christ at the climax of the assurance of the believer's security.
    - The intercession is not that of a nervous petitioner but of a victorious, enthroned king who pleads from a position of accomplished redemption.
    - When we sin or suffer, the response of Christ is not disappointment but intercession — he moves toward us, not away.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Intercession:** The act of pleading on behalf of another; Christ's heavenly intercession is the continuation of his priestly work begun on earth.
  - **Takeaway:** Every moment of a believer's life is covered by the ongoing, never-ceasing intercession of a compassionate High Priest.

- **Chapter 10 — Able to Save to the Uttermost**
  - **Main Idea:** Hebrews 7:25 promises that Christ is able to save "to the uttermost" — a phrase that speaks to both the depth and the completeness of his saving work.
  - **Key Points:**
    - "To the uttermost" (eis to panteles) can mean "completely" or "forever" — both senses are present and both are comforting.
    - The uttermost sinner, the uttermost backslider, and the uttermost sufferer all fall within the range of his saving ability.
    - This verse is not primarily about dramatic conversions but about the sustained, persevering salvation of ordinary, faltering believers.
    - The ability to save is grounded in the perpetual intercession — he saves fully because he intercedes always.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Eis to panteles:** Greek phrase meaning "to the uttermost," "completely," or "for all time"; found only here in the New Testament.
  - **Takeaway:** No believer is too far gone, too long backslidden, or too deeply broken to fall outside the reach of Christ's saving ability.

- **Chapter 11 — The Approach of God**
  - **Main Idea:** Throughout redemptive history, God consistently initiates — he seeks the lost, he comes down, he moves toward humanity — and this pattern culminates in the incarnation.
  - **Key Points:**
    - In Genesis 3, after the fall, God comes walking in the garden asking "Where are you?" — the seeking posture is primordial.
    - The exodus is God coming down to rescue a people who could not rescue themselves (Exodus 3:8).
    - The incarnation is the ultimate coming-down: the Son of God entering our condition to seek and save the lost (Luke 19:10).
    - Every act of divine approach in Scripture reinforces that God is not waiting for us to work our way to him but is himself on the move toward us.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Condescension (Theological):** God's gracious act of stooping to the level of the creature; in Reformed theology, all of God's covenantal dealings involve condescension.
  - **Takeaway:** The posture of God in Scripture is always leaning toward, never leaning away — he is the original seeker.

- **Chapter 12 — His Touch**
  - **Main Idea:** The repeated physical touch of Jesus in the healing narratives communicates something essential about his heart — he does not maintain clinical distance from the broken.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Jesus touched lepers, dead bodies, bleeding women, and blind men — all categories of ritual impurity — without becoming unclean himself.
    - In Christ, holiness is not contaminated by contact with sinners; rather, holiness contaminates and heals.
    - The touch is often unnecessary for the healing itself — it is an act of personal, embodied compassion beyond what the miracle required.
    - This pattern anticipates the resurrection body — in the new creation, Christ remains touchable, approachable, scarred (John 20:27).
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Ritual Impurity:** In the Old Testament system, a state of uncleanness that was contagious through contact; Jesus reverses the direction of contagion — his purity overwhelms impurity.
  - **Takeaway:** Jesus heals with his hands because touching the broken is an expression of his heart, not merely a mechanism of his power.

- **Chapter 13 — He Became Flesh**
  - **Main Idea:** John 1:14 — "the Word became flesh" — is the hinge of all Christology; the Son's assumption of human nature is permanent and eternally significant for how we relate to God.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The Word did not merely appear in flesh or visit flesh; he became flesh — a complete, irreversible identification with human nature.
    - The incarnation means God knows from the inside what hunger, exhaustion, grief, and temptation feel like.
    - The permanent humanity of the risen Christ (he still has a body, still bears the scars) means our mediator is forever one of us.
    - The tenderness of Christ toward human weakness is not a temporary attitude but is written into the eternal identity of the Son.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Hypostatic Union:** The theological term for the two natures — fully divine and fully human — united in one person, Jesus Christ, without confusion, change, division, or separation.
  - **Takeaway:** The incarnation is not a temporary measure God employed and then set aside; it is a permanent transformation that makes Christ our sympathetic mediator forever.

- **Chapter 14 — To the End**
  - **Main Idea:** John 13:1 — "having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end" — captures the persevering, non-withdrawing nature of Christ's love.
  - **Key Points:**
    - "To the end" (eis telos) means both "to the uttermost" and "to the very last moment" — completeness and duration.
    - The context is the Last Supper, the night of betrayal — Jesus loved Judas, Peter (who would deny him), and all who would abandon him.
    - Persevering love is not naive; Jesus knows what his disciples will do and loves them anyway.
    - This love is the pattern for Christian community (John 13:34–35), but it begins as a description of Christ's own heart.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Eis telos:** Greek phrase meaning "to the end" or "to the uttermost"; carries both temporal and qualitative force.
  - **Takeaway:** Christ's love does not have an exit clause triggered by our failures — it is the kind of love that goes all the way to the cross and beyond.

- **Chapter 15 — Crowning the Righteous and Receiving Sinners**
  - **Main Idea:** The same Christ who will one day crown the righteous is the one who receives sinners now — both acts flow from the same heart of grace.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Paul's confidence in 2 Timothy 4:8 that Christ will award him the crown of righteousness rests entirely on Christ's own righteousness imputed to him.
    - The future glory does not make Christ less accessible in the present; if anything, the certainty of future grace should make us more bold to approach now.
    - The parable of the workers in Matthew 20 reveals a God whose generosity cannot be constrained by human notions of merit.
    - Both the receiving of sinners and the crowning of the righteous are acts of divine grace, not divine reward for human achievement.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Imputed Righteousness:** The forensic act by which the righteousness of Christ is credited to the believer's account; the ground of both justification and future glorification.
  - **Takeaway:** The Jesus who will crown us on the last day is the same Jesus who receives us now in all our messiness.

- **Chapter 16 — Our Advocate**
  - **Main Idea:** 1 John 2:1–2 presents Christ as our advocate (parakletos) with the Father, and this advocacy is grounded in the propitiation he accomplished — he speaks for us from a place of finished work.
  - **Key Points:**
    - "If anyone sins, we have an advocate" — the first-class conditional assumes the reality of post-conversion sin; John is not shocked that believers sin.
    - The advocate does not plead our innocence but pleads his own sacrifice — the ground of the defense is the cross.
    - Satan is the accuser (Revelation 12:10); Christ is the advocate — two opposing postures toward the believer before God.
    - Every sin that produces shame and accusation has already been answered by the advocate.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Parakletos:** Greek word meaning advocate, helper, or one called alongside; used of both the Holy Spirit (John 14–16) and of Christ (1 John 2:1).
    - **Propitiation:** The satisfaction of divine wrath through the sacrifice of Christ; the ground on which the advocate pleads.
  - **Takeaway:** When you sin and the accuser points to your failure, the advocate points to his blood — and the blood speaks louder.

- **Chapter 17 — Yearning Jealousy**
  - **Main Idea:** James 4:5 speaks of God yearning jealously over the spirit he has placed in us — this divine jealousy is not petty but the jealousy of a lover who refuses to share his beloved with rivals.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The jealousy of God in Scripture is always covenantal — it is the jealousy of a husband for his wife, a father for his children, a shepherd for his sheep.
    - When believers drift toward the world (James 4:4), the divine response is not cold anger but hot jealousy — a yearning to recover what is being lost.
    - This jealousy is evidence that God is personally invested in the believer, not disengaged.
    - The appropriate response to divine jealousy is repentance and return, and James immediately offers the promise: "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you."
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Covenant Jealousy:** The zeal of God for the exclusive loyalty of his covenant partner; modeled on the marital relationship and expressed throughout the prophets.
  - **Takeaway:** God's jealousy over his people is not a threat but a love signal — he wants you too much to let you go without a fight.

- **Chapter 18 — The Explicitness of His Mercy**
  - **Main Idea:** In the Old Testament, God repeatedly names and describes his own mercy in first-person terms — he is not merely merciful in action but in explicit self-declaration.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Exodus 34:6–7 — "The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger..." — is God's own name for himself, his self-given identity.
    - This self-declaration at Sinai is quoted or alluded to more than any other passage in the Old Testament, suggesting it is foundational to Israel's theology.
    - The mercy comes first in the list — before holiness, before justice — as the primary note of the divine self-disclosure.
    - When the prophets plead for mercy, they appeal to this self-disclosure, holding God to his own name.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Thirteen Attributes:** The rabbinic tradition that counted thirteen divine attributes in Exodus 34:6–7; they are used as a paradigm for prayer throughout the Psalms and Prophets.
  - **Takeaway:** God has gone on record about his own character, and what he says about himself first is mercy — this is the God we approach.

- **Chapter 19 — The Saints' Experience of His Heart**
  - **Main Idea:** The Psalms are the record of the saints' experiential encounter with the heart of God, and they overwhelmingly return to themes of divine tenderness, patience, and rescue.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Psalm 103 catalogs the mercies of God in personal terms: "who forgives all your iniquity, who heals all your diseases" — the second-person address is intimate.
    - Psalm 51 (David after his sin with Bathsheba) reveals that even the worst moral failure does not forfeit access to God's mercy.
    - Psalm 34:18 — "The Lord is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit" — locates God specifically with the emotionally devastated.
    - The Psalms normalize lament, complaint, and raw honesty before God — the saints did not hide their darkness, and God did not turn away.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Lament:** A genre of prayer that expresses grief, confusion, and complaint to God; nearly a third of the Psalms are laments, and they are offered without apology.
  - **Takeaway:** The Psalms are proof that honesty about suffering does not drive God away — it invites his nearness.

- **Chapter 20 — The Law Written on Our Hearts**
  - **Main Idea:** The new covenant promise of Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36 — that God will write his law on hearts and give a new spirit — is the fulfillment of God's longing to be fully known and fully embraced by his people.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Jeremiah 31:33 — "I will put my law within them and write it on their hearts" — is not merely about obedience but about intimacy; the law internalized changes the relationship.
    - Ezekiel 36:26 — "I will give you a new heart and a new spirit" — frames regeneration as a divine gift, not a human achievement.
    - The new covenant is not merely the old covenant improved; it is a transformation of the relational structure itself.
    - The Spirit who writes the law on the heart is also the one who bears witness that we are children of God (Romans 8:16) — law and love converge.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **New Covenant:** The covenant inaugurated by Christ's blood (Luke 22:20) fulfilling the promises of Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36; characterized by internalized law, full forgiveness, and universal knowledge of God.
  - **Takeaway:** The new covenant is the institutional expression of what the heart of God has always wanted — full, intimate, unmediated relationship with his people.

- **Chapter 21 — All That Is Not Sin**
  - **Main Idea:** The Puritan tradition, particularly John Owen, argued that Christ sympathizes with every human infirmity and weakness that is not itself sinful — he meets us in our limits, not only our moral failures.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Hebrews 4:15 distinguishes between weakness/infirmity and sin — Christ sympathizes with the former even where he is distinct from the latter.
    - Exhaustion, grief, loneliness, physical pain, and confusion are not sins, and Christ entered all of them in the incarnation.
    - The tendency to spiritualize all suffering as the product of specific sin misreads both Scripture and the example of Jesus himself.
    - The comfort of Hebrews 4 is specifically for those who feel their weakness, not only those who feel their guilt.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Infirmity:** Weakness or limitation that does not arise from moral failure; Hebrews 4:15 uses the term to distinguish the zone of Christ's sympathy from his sinlessness.
  - **Takeaway:** Christ meets us not only in the mess of our sin but in the ordinary fragility of being human — he has been there too.

- **Chapter 22 — A Tender High Priest**
  - **Main Idea:** The climax of the high priest argument in Hebrews is that the priest-king who serves at the throne of grace does so with personal knowledge of what it costs to be human, and this gives the "draw near" invitation its power.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Hebrews 5:2 says the high priest can "deal gently" with the ignorant and wayward because he himself is beset with weakness.
    - In Christ, this principle reaches its highest expression — his gentleness is not compromised by his own sins (he has none) but is perfected by his own suffering.
    - "Dealt gently" translates metriopathein — to moderate one's passions, to hold back full judgment, to respond with measured tenderness.
    - Every approach to the throne of grace is met not by a disinterested bureaucrat but by a priest who knows.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Metriopathein:** Greek verb meaning to deal gently or to moderate one's emotions in response to another's failure; the priestly virtue described in Hebrews 5:2.
  - **Takeaway:** The one who judges us is also the one who suffered for us — and that combination produces a throne where grace, not terror, reigns.

- **Chapter 23 — His Heart is Not Changed by Our Sin**
  - **Main Idea:** The concluding argument of the book is that neither our sin nor our suffering changes the heart of Christ toward us — he is the same yesterday, today, and forever (Hebrews 13:8) in his gentleness.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Sin produces shame, and shame tells us to hide from God — but the Gospel says the very place we should go when we sin is to Christ, whose heart is gentle and lowly.
    - The cross is the permanent and irrevocable demonstration that Christ's heart toward sinners is one of self-giving love; that event does not become less true when we fail.
    - Hebrews 13:8 — "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever" — is a statement about his character, not merely his deity.
    - The Christian life is not a series of performances after which Christ approves or disapproves; it is a sustained relationship with one whose heart never wavers.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Immutability:** The divine attribute of unchangeableness; applied to Christ's character in Hebrews 13:8, it means his love and gentleness are not subject to fluctuation.
  - **Takeaway:** What you most need to know when you are at your worst is that Christ's heart toward you has not changed — and it never will.
