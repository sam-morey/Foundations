## TL;DR
- Graeme Goldsworthy traces the entire Bible through the lens of the kingdom of God, defined as "God's people in God's place under God's rule."
- Every stage of biblical history — from Eden to the new creation — is a progressive, typological revelation of this kingdom pattern, reaching its climax in Jesus Christ.
- Biblical theology is the discipline that reads Scripture as a unified, Christ-centered story, and Goldsworthy provides the clearest accessible introduction to this method.

## Source Info
- Title: *According to Plan: The Unfolding Revelation of God in the Bible*
- Author: Graeme Goldsworthy
- Publication Date: 1991 (revised edition widely used)
- Themes: Kingdom of God, Covenant, Prophecy & Fulfillment, Messiah & Christ, Redemption & Salvation

## Key Ideas
- The kingdom of God — God's people in God's place under God's rule — is the organizing principle of the entire Bible and provides the lens through which every biblical text finds its meaning.
- Biblical history moves in a series of stages, each of which embodies the kingdom pattern in a typological form that points forward to its fulfillment in Jesus Christ.
- The New Testament does not replace the Old Testament but interprets it — Christ is the key that unlocks the meaning of every Old Testament person, event, institution, and prophecy.
- Biblical theology (reading the Bible as a historically unfolding, Christ-centered revelation) is the foundation for all other theological disciplines and for faithful Christian living.

## Chapter Summaries
- **Part One: Introduction to Biblical Theology**

- **Chapter 1: The Territory Ahead**
  - **Main Idea:** Biblical theology is the study of the Bible on its own terms as a historically developing, unified revelation that moves from promise to fulfillment in Christ.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Many Christians read the Bible as a collection of stories, moral lessons, or proof texts rather than as a unified story.
    - Biblical theology reads each text in its historical context within the unfolding narrative of the whole Bible.
    - The goal of biblical theology is to see how each part of Scripture relates to Christ, who is the center and climax of the whole.
    - Goldsworthy introduces the "kingdom of God" as the central theme that binds the two testaments together.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Biblical theology:** The discipline that studies the progressive, historically unfolding revelation of God in Scripture as a unified whole that finds its center in Jesus Christ.
    - **Kingdom of God:** Goldsworthy's central organizing category — "God's people in God's place under God's rule."
  - **Takeaway:** Reading the Bible as a unified, Christ-centered story is not a technique but the right way to read it on its own terms.

- **Chapter 2: How Do We Know?**
  - **Main Idea:** All human knowing is shaped by prior commitments, and the Christian's epistemology begins with the triune God who has spoken in Scripture.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Epistemology (the question of how we know) is not a neutral philosophical exercise but is shaped by one's worldview commitments.
    - Christians are not less rational than secularists; they operate from a different, and more coherent, starting point.
    - Revelation — God's speech — is the foundation of all true knowledge for the Christian.
    - The Bible's authority is self-authenticating and is received by faith, not established by external proofs alone.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Epistemology:** The branch of philosophy concerned with how we know what we know and what counts as valid knowledge.
    - **Revelation:** God's self-disclosure — in creation, in Scripture, and supremely in Jesus Christ.
  - **Takeaway:** Christian thought begins with God's word as the foundation of knowledge, not as one option among many.

- **Chapter 3: God's Plan in the Bible**
  - **Main Idea:** The Bible presents a unified divine plan — not a collection of disconnected messages — and biblical theology is the discipline of tracing that plan through Scripture.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Scripture itself testifies to a unified divine purpose unfolding through history.
    - The plan is not invented by theologians reading backward but is explicitly stated in key texts (Ephesians 1:9–10; Romans 16:25–26; Colossians 1:25–27).
    - The unity of the Bible means that the interpreter cannot understand any part correctly in isolation from the whole.
    - Goldsworthy introduces the framework of promise and fulfillment as the backbone of the biblical plan.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Promise and fulfillment:** The pattern by which Old Testament texts anticipate (promise) what New Testament texts announce as accomplished (fulfillment) in Christ.
    - **Salvation history (Heilsgeschichte):** The historical sequence of God's saving acts — the concrete events through which God's plan unfolds.
  - **Takeaway:** The Bible is not a random anthology but the record of a single, coherent divine plan moving toward its goal in Jesus Christ.

- **Chapter 4: The Kingdom of God — Our Interpretive Key**
  - **Main Idea:** The kingdom of God — God's people, in God's place, under God's rule — is the organizing theme that enables the interpreter to trace the unity of the whole Bible.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The kingdom formula ("God's people in God's place under God's rule") captures the essential structure of the relationship between God and his people at every stage of biblical history.
    - This pattern first appears in Eden, is progressively redefined through Israel's history, and reaches its fulfillment in Christ and the new creation.
    - Using this lens, the interpreter can see how each stage of biblical history relates to every other stage.
    - The kingdom is not merely a future hope but a present reality inaugurated by Jesus — with a future consummation still to come.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Typology:** The relationship between an earlier biblical pattern (type) and its later, greater fulfillment (antitype) — the primary way the Old Testament points to Christ.
    - **Kingdom pattern:** The recurring structure of God's people, God's place, and God's rule that organizes the whole Bible.
  - **Takeaway:** The kingdom of God is not one theme among many in the Bible — it is the theme that makes all the others cohere.

- **Part Two: The Old Testament**

- **Chapter 5: Kingdom and Creation (Genesis 1–2)**
  - **Main Idea:** Creation is the original, archetypal expression of the kingdom — God's good world with his image-bearing people in the perfect place, under his wise and good rule.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Eden is the first expression of "God's people in God's place under God's rule" — the template that every subsequent stage of the kingdom develops.
    - The image of God defines humanity's royal vocation: to represent God and extend his rule over creation.
    - The Sabbath at creation's end points forward to the rest and completion that is the kingdom's ultimate goal.
    - Creation's goodness is foundational: the plan of God is not to discard the world but to renew it.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Image of God:** Humanity's unique dignity as God's royal representatives, called to reflect his character and exercise delegated rule over creation.
    - **Sabbath:** The seventh day of creation — a pattern of rest pointing forward to the eschatological goal of God's completed kingdom.
  - **Takeaway:** Eden is the prototype of the kingdom; everything that follows is the story of how that prototype is lost, promised, and finally restored in Christ.

- **Chapter 6: Kingdom and Rebellion (Genesis 3–11)**
  - **Main Idea:** The Fall fractures every dimension of the kingdom — the people rebel, the place is cursed, and the rule of God is rejected — establishing the problem the rest of the Bible addresses.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Sin is fundamentally a rejection of God's rule — the desire to be autonomous rather than dependent and obedient.
    - The consequences spread: shame, judgment, expulsion from Eden (the loss of God's place), violence, and social breakdown.
    - Even in judgment, God makes a promise (Genesis 3:15) — the seed of the woman who will crush the serpent.
    - The Babel narrative shows sin operating at a civilizational scale — the human project of self-rule apart from God.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Protoevangelium:** The first gospel — the promise in Genesis 3:15 of the coming seed who will defeat the serpent; the first ray of light in the story's darkness.
    - **Common grace:** God's unmerited goodness to all humanity in restraining the full effects of sin and allowing civilization, culture, and life to continue.
  - **Takeaway:** The Fall does not end the plan — it raises the stakes and points the reader forward to the one who will restore what was lost.

- **Chapter 7: Kingdom and the Patriarchs (Genesis 12–50)**
  - **Main Idea:** God's call of Abraham is the beginning of the concrete answer to the Fall — a people chosen to be the vehicle of God's kingdom purposes for the whole world.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The Abrahamic covenant establishes the three elements of the kingdom: a people (Abraham's descendants), a place (Canaan), and a rule (relationship with God defined by covenant).
    - The covenant promises are unconditional — God will accomplish what he promised, regardless of Abraham's and his descendants' failures.
    - The patriarchal narratives show God sustaining his promise through human weakness, deception, and failure.
    - The kingdom pattern at this stage is embryonic — a family, not yet a nation; a promise, not yet a possession.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Abrahamic covenant:** God's binding promise to Abraham: land, descendants, and blessing for all nations through his offspring (Genesis 12, 15, 17).
    - **Typology of the patriarchs:** The way Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph each foreshadow aspects of Christ and the kingdom.
  - **Takeaway:** Abraham's call establishes the shape of the kingdom in promise form — the rest of the Old Testament is the progressive unfolding of these covenant promises.

- **Chapter 8: Kingdom and the Exodus (Exodus – Deuteronomy)**
  - **Main Idea:** The exodus is the decisive Old Testament act of redemption — God rescuing his people, giving them his law, and bringing them to his place — a pattern that typifies all of God's saving work.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The exodus is the paradigmatic act of salvation in the Old Testament: God sees, hears, comes down, and rescues.
    - Sinai establishes the covenant law — the shape of life for God's people under his rule.
    - The tabernacle is God's dwelling among his people — a portable Eden where God's presence is restored.
    - The exodus becomes the lens through which Israel (and the New Testament) understands all subsequent acts of salvation, including the greater exodus accomplished by Christ.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Passover:** The act of redemption by blood that delivers Israel from death — the type that points forward to Christ's atoning sacrifice.
    - **Tabernacle:** The portable sanctuary in which God dwells with Israel — a restored Eden, pointing forward to Christ (John 1:14) and ultimately to the new creation.
  - **Takeaway:** The exodus gives the entire Old Testament its vocabulary of redemption — and points forward to the greater exodus of the cross and resurrection.

- **Chapter 9: Kingdom and Conquest (Joshua – Judges)**
  - **Main Idea:** The conquest of Canaan is the fulfillment (in type) of the land promise — God's people in God's place — but Judges reveals how incomplete this fulfillment is without a faithful king.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Joshua's conquest of Canaan fulfills the land element of the Abrahamic covenant in its first, typological form.
    - The pattern of Judges — rebellion, oppression, cry, deliverance, rest, repeat — reveals the deep problem of Israel's heart.
    - The judges are temporary, flawed deliverers who point forward to the need for a permanent, faithful king.
    - The land itself is a type pointing to the greater "place" of God's presence in Christ and the new creation.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Typological fulfillment:** When an Old Testament promise is partially fulfilled in a way that points forward to a greater, final fulfillment in Christ.
    - **Judge:** A Spirit-empowered deliverer raised up by God to rescue Israel — a type of the coming Messiah.
  - **Takeaway:** The conquest gives Israel the land but not the heart — the need for a greater king who will bring the true and final rest becomes clear.

- **Chapter 10: Kingdom and Kingship (1 Samuel – 2 Samuel; 1 Kings 1–11)**
  - **Main Idea:** The Davidic monarchy represents the most complete expression of the kingdom in the Old Testament — and its establishment, failure, and promise of a coming heir set the stage for the messianic expectation.
  - **Key Points:**
    - David's kingdom — God's people in the land, with God's king, under God's rule — is the high point of the kingdom in the Old Testament.
    - The Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7) promises an eternal throne and a son who will be God's son — the foundation of messianic expectation.
    - Solomon's reign begins in wisdom and glory but ends in idolatry — showing that even the best earthly king cannot finally establish the kingdom.
    - The whole monarchy becomes a "type" pointing to the one true King who will succeed where all human kings fail.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Davidic covenant:** God's unconditional promise to David of an eternal dynasty and a throne that will endure — fulfilled ultimately in Jesus (2 Samuel 7; Luke 1:32–33).
    - **Messianic typology:** The way the Davidic kings function as types of the coming Messiah — their victories, failures, and promises all pointing forward.
  - **Takeaway:** David and Solomon at their best show what the kingdom looks like; their failures show why a greater son of David is needed.

- **Chapter 11: Kingdom and Decline (1 Kings 12 – 2 Kings 25)**
  - **Main Idea:** The division, deterioration, and exile of Israel demonstrate that the kingdom cannot be sustained by human faithfulness — only God can establish it permanently.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The divided kingdom (Israel and Judah) represents the fragmentation of the Davidic ideal.
    - The prophets arise precisely during this period — calling Israel back to covenant faithfulness and pointing forward to the restoration God will bring.
    - The exile (Babylon) is the catastrophic consequence of covenant unfaithfulness — losing both the place and the king.
    - Yet the prophets consistently frame the exile as a new exodus — the worst disaster becomes the context for the greatest promises of restoration.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Exile:** The deportation of Israel and Judah to Assyria and Babylon — the historical low point that generates the Old Testament's most profound promises of restoration.
    - **Remnant:** The faithful minority within Israel through whom God preserves his covenant purposes — pointing to the church as the true people of God.
  - **Takeaway:** The exile proves that the kingdom cannot be built on human faithfulness — only God's sovereign, gracious act can restore what has been lost.

- **Chapter 12: Kingdom and Prophecy**
  - **Main Idea:** The prophets interpret Israel's history in light of the covenant and point forward to a new exodus, a new covenant, a new creation, and a new king — all fulfilled in Jesus.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The prophets are covenant lawyers, not merely predictors of the future — they apply the Mosaic covenant to their generation and announce consequences.
    - The great prophetic promises (Isaiah's Servant Songs, Jeremiah's new covenant, Ezekiel's new temple, Daniel's Son of Man) all converge on the coming Messiah.
    - The prophetic vision consistently moves from judgment to restoration — the exile is not the end of the story.
    - The "Day of the LORD" is the key eschatological concept in the prophets — the day God will judge evil and establish his kingdom fully.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Servant of the LORD:** Isaiah's figure (Isaiah 42, 49, 50, 53) who suffers vicariously and accomplishes the new exodus — fulfilled in Jesus.
    - **New covenant:** Jeremiah's promise (Jeremiah 31:31–34) of an inward, Spirit-written covenant that will achieve what the Mosaic covenant could not.
  - **Takeaway:** The prophets are the bridge between the Old Testament's partially fulfilled kingdom and the New Testament's announcement of its arrival in Christ.

- **Chapter 13: Wisdom, Psalms, and the Kingdom**
  - **Main Idea:** The Wisdom literature and Psalms explore the life of the kingdom from the inside — what it looks like to live faithfully before God in every dimension of experience.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The Psalms present the full range of human experience before God — praise, lament, trust, penitence — all within the covenant framework.
    - The royal Psalms (Psalm 2, 45, 72, 110) celebrate the Davidic king and point forward to the Messiah.
    - Wisdom literature (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Job) explores how to live well in God's world — grounded in the "fear of the LORD" as the beginning of wisdom.
    - Even the difficulties of Ecclesiastes and Job fit within the kingdom framework: the failure of human wisdom to find ultimate meaning points to the need for divine revelation.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Fear of the LORD:** The foundational disposition of reverence, trust, and obedience toward God that is the beginning of wisdom and the heart of kingdom life.
    - **Royal Psalms:** Psalms that celebrate Israel's king and, by extension, point forward to the coming Messianic King.
  - **Takeaway:** The Writings give texture to kingdom life — showing what faithful humanity looks like in worship, work, suffering, and daily living.

- **Part Three: The New Testament**

- **Chapter 14: Kingdom and John the Baptist**
  - **Main Idea:** John the Baptist is the final Old Testament prophet — the forerunner who announces that the long-awaited kingdom is about to arrive.
  - **Key Points:**
    - John stands at the hinge of the Testaments — he belongs to the old age that is passing even as he announces the new.
    - His ministry of repentance and baptism prepares Israel for the arrival of the King.
    - John's imprisonment and death foreshadow the pattern of the cross: the kingdom comes through suffering.
    - Jesus identifies John as Elijah returned — the eschatological prophet of the end times (Malachi 4:5).
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Forerunner:** One who goes ahead to prepare the way — John's role in relation to Jesus, fulfilling Isaiah 40 and Malachi 3.
    - **Eschatological prophet:** A prophet who announces the arrival of the end times — John fills this role as the last of the old covenant prophets.
  - **Takeaway:** John's ministry marks the end of the long period of promise and the beginning of the moment of fulfillment.

- **Chapter 15: Jesus and the Kingdom**
  - **Main Idea:** In Jesus, the kingdom of God arrives in person — he is simultaneously the proclaimer, the embodiment, and the inaugurator of God's reign.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Jesus' announcement "The kingdom of God is at hand" is not merely a statement about the future but a declaration that God's reign has arrived in his own person.
    - Jesus' miracles are kingdom acts — signs that God's new creation has broken into the old order.
    - Jesus fulfills every Old Testament type: he is the true Adam, the greater Moses, the faithful Israel, the son of David, the suffering Servant.
    - The cross and resurrection are not interruptions of the kingdom program but its decisive accomplishment.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Fulfillment:** The New Testament concept that in Jesus, every Old Testament promise, pattern, and type reaches its intended goal.
    - **Christological typology:** The reading of Old Testament types as pointing forward to Christ as their ultimate referent and fulfillment.
  - **Takeaway:** Jesus is the kingdom in person — the point at which every thread of the Old Testament story arrives.

- **Chapter 16: The Kingdom Proclaimed**
  - **Main Idea:** The apostolic preaching in Acts and the New Testament letters proclaims that Jesus is Lord and that his resurrection has inaugurated the kingdom — calling all people to submit to the King.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Pentecost launches the kingdom community into the world with the Spirit's power.
    - The apostolic proclamation (kerygma) is the announcement of what God has done in Jesus, not primarily a set of moral demands.
    - The letters interpret the significance of Christ's death and resurrection for the life of the new covenant community.
    - The church is the eschatological community — living in the time between the inaugurated and consummated kingdom.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Kerygma:** The apostolic proclamation — the core announcement of Christ's death, resurrection, exaltation, and coming return.
    - **Eschatological community:** The church as the community of the last days — living between resurrection and return, embodying the kingdom now.
  - **Takeaway:** The apostolic mission is the extension of Jesus' kingdom announcement into all the world through the Spirit-empowered church.

- **Chapter 17: The Kingdom Consummated**
  - **Main Idea:** The kingdom that began in creation, was promised through Israel, arrived in Jesus, and is proclaimed by the church will be fully consummated at Christ's return — the new creation.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The return of Christ (parousia) is the event at which the kingdom is finally and fully established.
    - The resurrection of the dead, the judgment, and the renewal of creation complete the kingdom pattern: God's people, in God's renewed place, under God's perfect rule.
    - Revelation presents the consummation in richly symbolic imagery — the New Jerusalem as the perfected city of God.
    - The consummation does not leave the physical creation behind but transforms and renews it.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Parousia:** The Greek term for Christ's return — his royal arrival to complete the establishment of God's kingdom.
    - **New creation:** The final renewal of the entire cosmos — heaven and earth joined, death abolished, God dwelling with his people forever.
  - **Takeaway:** The plan God announced in creation, and which the whole Bible traces, will be fully accomplished — and it will be better than Eden.

- **Conclusion: Reading the Bible According to Plan**
  - **Main Idea:** Biblical theology equips Christians to read every part of Scripture in relation to Christ and the kingdom — and to live faithfully in light of the whole story.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Every biblical text — Old and New Testament — finds its ultimate meaning in relation to Christ.
    - Reading the Bible "according to plan" does not collapse its diversity but sees each part's contribution to the whole.
    - Biblical theology is not an academic luxury but a pastoral necessity — it shapes how Christians understand their identity, calling, and hope.
    - The "according to plan" method: ask of every text where it fits in the unfolding kingdom story and how it points to Christ.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Christocentric hermeneutic:** Interpreting Scripture with Christ as the interpretive center — reading the Old Testament as pointing forward to him and the New Testament as explaining his significance.
    - **Kingdom of God as interpretive lens:** Using the pattern of "God's people in God's place under God's rule" to trace the Bible's unity across both Testaments.
  - **Takeaway:** To read the Bible according to plan is to see every text in the light of Christ — and to find that the whole Scripture proclaims the same glorious kingdom.
