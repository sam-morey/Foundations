## TL;DR
- *Till We Have Faces* is C. S. Lewis's retelling of the Cupid and Psyche myth from the perspective of Orual, the ugly queen of Glome, whose obsessive love for her beautiful sister Psyche drives the whole tragedy.
- The novel is structured as Orual's complaint against the gods—a furious accusation that they deal unjustly—but as she writes, the complaint gradually undoes itself, revealing that what she called love was really possession, jealousy, and self-will.
- The title names the book's deepest question: we cannot meet the gods, or be honestly known by them or by ourselves, until we have a real face to show—until we have become honest about who we are.

## Source Info
- Title: *Till We Have Faces: A Myth Retold*
- Author: C. S. Lewis
- Publication Date: 1956
- Themes:
  - Love and possessiveness
  - Faith and trust
  - Judgment and self-knowledge
  - Redemption and salvation
  - Sin and the Fall
  - The nature of the gods and divine hiddenness

## Key Ideas
- Possessive love—love that cannot let the beloved go free, cannot rejoice in the beloved's joy unless it is also the lover's possession—is not truly love at all but a subtle form of self-worship.
- The gods' seeming silence and hiddenness are not cruelty; the problem is that our noise, our complaints, our self-justifications, drown out the answer we do not want to hear.
- Orual's unveiling—her encounter with the divine at the end—is only possible after the veil of self-deception has been stripped away; judgment and mercy arrive together.
- Lewis uses the pagan myth deliberately: natural, half-understood religion and longing are both honored and corrected by the fuller story of sacrifice, death, and divine love that the myth anticipates.

## Chapter Summaries

- **Part One, Chapter 1 — Orual Begins Her Complaint**
  - **Main Idea:** Orual, queen of Glome, announces that she is writing her book as an indictment of the gods, who have ruined her life and the life of those she loved.
  - **Key Points:**
    - She introduces herself as ugly, a queen, and a woman who has learned to live without beauty or softness.
    - She frames the book explicitly as a legal case: she will set down the facts and demand that the gods answer.
    - The reader is already primed to sense that Orual may be an unreliable narrator of her own story.
  - **Key Quotes:**
    - "I will accuse the gods, especially the god who lives on the Grey Mountain."
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Glome:** The fictional barbarian kingdom over which Orual rules, loosely set in the ancient world near Greece.
  - **Takeaway:** The book opens as a courtroom complaint, but the shape of the narrative will eventually reverse the direction of judgment.

- **Part One, Chapters 2–4 — The Fox and the Sisters**
  - **Main Idea:** Orual describes her childhood, the arrival of the Greek slave Fox who becomes her tutor, and the birth of Psyche—the beautiful sister whom Orual will love more than anyone.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The Fox represents Greek rationalism and philosophy; he gives Orual intellectual formation but his worldview has limits the story will expose.
    - Psyche is radiant from birth—everyone who sees her is drawn to her beauty, which eventually attracts both worship and disaster.
    - Orual's love for Psyche is intense and maternal; she is both protector and, already, beginning to cling.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **The Fox:** A Greek slave and tutor who teaches Orual philosophy, reason, and rhetoric; a voice for the Stoic-rational worldview.
    - **Psyche (Istra):** Orual's much younger, beautiful half-sister, around whom the myth's central events revolve.
  - **Takeaway:** The bond between Orual and Psyche is the emotional center of the novel, but it is marked from the start by imbalance—Orual's love is consuming even when it is genuine.

- **Part One, Chapters 5–8 — The Offering to Ungit**
  - **Main Idea:** A plague and famine strike Glome, and the priest of Ungit declares that the land's curse can only be lifted by sacrificing the Great Offering—a person of royal blood given to the Shadowbrute on the holy mountain.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Ungit is the barbaric mother-goddess of Glome, a figure connected to fertility, blood sacrifice, and the dark side of religious awe; she is the local form of Aphrodite.
    - Psyche is chosen as the Great Offering, partly because her beauty has led the people to near-worship of her—profaning the goddess.
    - Orual is devastated and furious; she attempts to bribe or argue Psyche free, and fails.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **Ungit:** The dark, shapeless goddess of Glome—a figure combining elements of Aphrodite, earth-religion, and fear-based worship.
    - **The Great Offering:** The ritual sacrifice of a noble victim, left bound on the mountain for the Shadowbrute, representing the ambiguity of sacrifice in pre-Christian religion.
  - **Takeaway:** The sacrifice of the innocent, and the question of whether the gods demand or permit such things, is the first great test of faith in the novel.

- **Part One, Chapters 9–11 — Psyche on the Mountain**
  - **Main Idea:** When Orual secretly goes to the holy mountain to recover Psyche's bones, she finds Psyche alive—healthy, happy, and seemingly living in a palace that Orual cannot see.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Psyche claims she has been taken as the bride of a god, lives in a beautiful palace, and is happy beyond description.
    - Orual can see only a barren mountainside and a stream; she cannot perceive the palace at all, or only glimpses it for one uncertain moment.
    - Psyche asks only one thing: that Orual trust her, believe her, and leave her in peace with the god.
  - **Key Quotes:**
    - "Are you so sure that the god is not here, that what I have found is not real?"
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **The Palace:** The invisible dwelling of the god, perceived by Psyche and glimpsed uncertainly by Orual—a figure for spiritual reality that cannot be verified by reason or sight alone.
  - **Takeaway:** Orual is given a direct invitation to faith—to trust Psyche's testimony about the unseen—and she refuses it, beginning the novel's central tragedy.

- **Part One, Chapters 12–14 — Orual's Temptation and Betrayal**
  - **Main Idea:** Unable to trust what she cannot see, and unable to bear Psyche's happiness apart from herself, Orual devises a plan to force Psyche to disobey the god and reveal his face.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Orual argues herself into believing she is acting out of love and concern for Psyche's safety.
    - She threatens Psyche with her own death—she will kill herself if Psyche does not comply—a form of emotional coercion dressed as sacrifice.
    - Psyche, believing her sister's threat is genuine, obeys; she lights the lamp and looks at the god's face.
  - **Key Quotes:**
    - "I did not know then that what I was doing to Psyche was what Ungit did to all who came near her."
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **The Lamp:** The instrument of Psyche's act of disobedience—demanded by Orual's refusal to trust—a symbol of the way human insistence on seeing and controlling destroys what it claims to love.
  - **Takeaway:** Orual's love, at its worst, is indistinguishable from the possessive jealousy of the dark goddess she despises.

- **Part One, Chapters 15–17 — The Curse and Its Aftermath**
  - **Main Idea:** After Psyche's disobedience the god banishes her, the palace vanishes, and a divine voice tells Orual she will come to know herself—a curse and a promise at once.
  - **Key Points:**
    - The god pronounces judgment not on Psyche alone but also on Orual, who is named as the true cause of the disaster.
    - Orual returns to Glome and, over the following decades, becomes a capable, hard, and veiled queen—she begins wearing a veil over her ugly face and never removes it.
    - Her life becomes a monument to grief, work, and the suppression of all feeling; she is outwardly successful and inwardly hollowed.
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **The Veil:** Orual's literal and symbolic covering—she hides her face from others as she hides her true self from herself; it becomes a mark of her unresolved self-deception.
  - **Takeaway:** The veil is a powerful image of the self that cannot face either God or neighbor honestly, and of the way grief and guilt can be managed rather than healed.

- **Part One, Chapters 18–20 — Queen of Glome**
  - **Main Idea:** Orual describes her years as queen: wise, just, and successful as a ruler, but haunted by Psyche and by the void of her inner life.
  - **Key Points:**
    - She learns to govern well, to fight, and to earn the respect of her people—the Fox calls her the best of his students.
    - She encounters versions of Psyche's story circulating as a myth in the Greek world, and is enraged that the myth makes her the villain.
    - She writes partly to correct the record—but the process of writing begins to uncover what she has suppressed.
  - **Takeaway:** External virtue and public achievement can be the most refined form of self-concealment; Orual has buried the truth under decades of busyness and competence.

- **Part Two — The Vision and the Unmaking**
  - **Main Idea:** In a series of visions, Orual is forced to see her own heart clearly for the first time; she witnesses Psyche completing her divine tasks and finally reads her own complaint aloud before a divine tribunal.
  - **Key Points:**
    - In the visions, Orual sees that she herself is Ungit—the devouring, possessive, self-centered love—and recognizes the same hunger in herself that she despised in the goddess.
    - When she reads her complaint aloud before the gods, she discovers it has shrunk to a few repeated, ugly sentences; the elaborate case has dissolved into the bare core of self-will.
    - Psyche appears at last, transformed and beautiful beyond description, completing her tasks—not as victim but as one who has passed through suffering into glory.
  - **Key Quotes:**
    - "I saw well why the gods do not speak to us openly, nor let us answer. Till that word can be dug out of us, why should they hear the babble that we think we mean?"
    - "Lightly men talk of saying what they mean. Often when he was teaching me to write in Greek the Fox would say, 'Child, to say the very thing you really mean, the whole of it, nothing more or less or other than what you really mean; that's the whole art and joy of words.'"
  - **Defined Terms:**
    - **"I am Ungit":** The moment of Orual's recognition that the possessive, consuming love she attributed to the goddess is the love she herself practiced toward Psyche.
    - **Psyche's Tasks:** The divine labors Psyche must complete after her banishment, which in Lewis's version are witnessed by Orual in vision—Psyche suffers partly because of what Orual made her do.
  - **Takeaway:** True self-knowledge—seeing oneself as one truly is before God—is both the most painful and the most liberating experience in the book; it is the precondition of receiving a face.

- **Epilogue — Till We Have Faces**
  - **Main Idea:** The book closes with Orual's final entry, written after her visions, in which she understands that the god's silence was not cruelty, and that she has been answered in the only way that could have helped her.
  - **Key Points:**
    - Orual dies at peace, having seen Psyche's glory and been seen herself by the god.
    - The answer the gods give is not a verbal response to her complaint but a transformation of the one making the complaint.
    - The question "till we have faces" is answered: she now has a face—she has become real, honest, and capable of being truly known.
  - **Key Quotes:**
    - "I ended my first book with the words no answer. I know now, Lord, why you utter no answer. You are yourself the answer. Before your face questions die away."
  - **Takeaway:** The book's movement is from accusation to confession, from self-justification to self-knowledge, from a face hidden under a veil to a face that can stand before the divine.
