## Biblical Events
- ~330 BC — Alexander the Great arrives in Judea; Jewish high priest reportedly meets him at the gate; Temple worship continues
- ~301 BC — After Alexander's death, Judea passes to the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt (one of the successor states)
- ~250 BC — The Septuagint (LXX) translation begun in Alexandria; the Hebrew scriptures rendered into Greek for diaspora Jews
- ~198 BC — Seleucid King Antiochus III defeats the Ptolemies at the Battle of Panium; Judea passes to Seleucid control
- ~175 BC — Antiochus IV Epiphanes becomes king; begins aggressive Hellenization of Judea; replaces the high priest with compliant appointees Jason and Menelaus
- ~167 BC — Antiochus IV desecrates the Temple: sacrifices a pig on the altar, installs a statue of Zeus Olympius, bans Torah observance, Sabbath, and circumcision on pain of death ("the abomination of desolation," Dan 11:31; 1 Macc 1:54)
- ~167 BC — The Maccabean revolt begins; Mattathias the priest kills a Jew about to offer a pagan sacrifice, then flees to the wilderness with his sons
- ~166 BC — Judas Maccabeus leads the revolt; guerrilla warfare against Seleucid forces
- ~164 BC — Judas recaptures Jerusalem; cleanses and rededicates the Temple; institution of Hanukkah (1 Macc 4; John 10:22)
- ~142 BC — Simon Maccabeus achieves full political independence; the Hasmonean dynasty established
- ~63 BC — Pompey the Great enters Jerusalem; Judea becomes a Roman protectorate; the Hellenistic period ends

## World History Context

Alexander III of Macedon (356–323 BC), tutored by Aristotle and inspired by Homer's Achilles, conquered the Persian Empire in just seven years (334–327 BC), defeating Darius III at the battles of Granicus (334), Issus (333), and Gaugamela (331 BC). He founded the city of Alexandria in Egypt (331 BC), which would become the greatest intellectual center of the ancient world, home to its famous Library and Museum. Alexander died in Babylon in 323 BC at age 32, without a clear heir, and his vast empire was divided among his generals (the Diadochi, "successors") in the Wars of the Successors that followed.

The result was the Hellenistic world — a fusion of Greek (Hellenic) culture with the diverse civilizations of Egypt, Persia, and the Near East. Greek (koine, "common" Greek) became the lingua franca of commerce, literature, and administration from Egypt to Afghanistan. This linguistic reality is what made possible the New Testament: written in koine Greek, it could be read across the entire Mediterranean world. The Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, begun under Ptolemy II Philadelphus (~250 BC) in Alexandria — became the Bible of the early church and is extensively quoted throughout the New Testament.

The two successor kingdoms most relevant to biblical history were the Ptolemaic Kingdom (Egypt, controlling Judea 301–198 BC) and the Seleucid Kingdom (Syria/Mesopotamia, controlling Judea 198–63 BC). Daniel 11, written with extraordinary precision, describes these two kingdoms as "the king of the South" and "the king of the North" in a series of prophecies about their conflicts. The Ptolemaic period was generally tolerant of Jewish practice and produced a cosmopolitan Jewish culture in Alexandria (Philo, the Septuagint). The Seleucid period ended catastrophically with Antiochus IV Epiphanes, whose attempt to forcibly Hellenize the Jews — outlawing Torah observance, desecrating the Temple — provoked the Maccabean revolt, one of the few Jewish military victories recorded in the ancient record. The revolt produced the Hasmonean dynasty and, as an unexpected byproduct, the development of the major Jewish sects (Pharisees, Sadducees) whose debates fill the pages of the New Testament.

Meanwhile, Rome was growing from a regional Italian power into the dominant force of the Mediterranean. The three Punic Wars against Carthage (264–146 BC) resulted in Carthage's total destruction, making Rome master of the western Mediterranean. Simultaneously Rome absorbed Greece (Macedonia became a Roman province in 148 BC, mainland Greece in 146 BC) and was expanding steadily eastward toward Judea.

## Dominant Powers & Their Impact on Israel
| Power | Dates | Impact on Israel/Bible |
|-------|-------|------------------------|
| Macedonian/Greek Empire (Alexander) | 334–323 BC | Rapid conquest of Persia; Greek language and culture spread universally; Judea integrated into the Hellenistic world |
| Ptolemaic Kingdom (Egypt) | 323–198 BC | Relatively tolerant rule; Septuagint produced in Alexandria; Jewish diaspora flourishes in Egypt |
| Seleucid Kingdom (Syria) | 198–63 BC | Antiochus IV's persecution precipitates Maccabean revolt; the "abomination of desolation" and cleansing of the Temple |
| Hasmonean Dynasty (Jewish) | 142–63 BC | Jewish political independence; Pharisees, Sadducees, and Essenes emerge as distinct sects; coins minted; later dynastic infighting invites Rome |
| Roman Republic | 264–63 BC | Rising power in the west; alliance with Maccabees (1 Macc 8); Pompey takes Jerusalem 63 BC; end of Jewish independence |

## Key Archaeological Evidence
- **Dead Sea Scrolls** (c. 250 BC–68 AD, discovered 1947, Qumran caves, West Bank; now at the Shrine of the Book, Israel Museum, Jerusalem) — Over 900 manuscripts including every OT book except Esther; the Great Isaiah Scroll (complete, dated ~125 BC) is 1,000 years older than any previously known OT manuscript; community documents (Community Rule, War Scroll, Thanksgiving Hymns) describe the Essene sect that produced the scrolls; the copper scroll lists buried treasures
- **1 Maccabees** (composed c. 100 BC) — Historical narrative of the Maccabean revolt (175–134 BC) in the style of the Deuteronomistic History; highly reliable historical source corroborated by Josephus and Seleucid records; describes the desecration of the Temple, the revolt, and Hanukkah
- **Antiochus IV Epiphanes Coinage** — Greek tetradrachms bearing his portrait and the title "Theos Epiphanes" ("God Manifest") — the blasphemous divine pretension that made his persecution of Judaism particularly heinous
- **Hasmonean Coins** — Bronze coins from John Hyrcanus I through Antigonus Mattathias; the first Jewish coinage; inscriptions in Hebrew and Greek; demonstrate the cultural duality of the Hasmonean dynasty
- **Masada Archaeological Remains** (excavated by Yigael Yadin, 1963–1965) — Herodian fortress built on earlier Hasmonean foundations; Hebrew and Aramaic ostraca, synagogue remains, mikvehs (ritual baths), the Masada scroll fragments; provides a vivid picture of Jewish life in the late Hellenistic and early Roman periods
- **Scrolls at Nag Hammadi** (Egypt, 1945) — Coptic manuscripts including Gnostic texts; while later than this period, they reflect Hellenistic religious syncretism developing during the intertestamental centuries
- **Stele of Antiochus III at Jerusalem** (c. 198 BC) — Inscription granting Jews the right to live according to their ancestral laws following Seleucid conquest; shows the initial Seleucid tolerance before Antiochus IV's reversal
- **Rhodes Colossus (destroyed 226 BC) and other Hellenistic monuments** — The built environment of Hellenistic cities (Alexandria, Antioch, Pergamum) shaped the cultural world that produced both the Septuagint and, later, the New Testament

## Notable Figures (Biblical & Historical)
| Figure | Role | Significance |
|--------|------|--------------|
| Alexander the Great | Macedonian king; world conqueror | Spread Greek language universally, making the NT possible; his death without an heir fulfilled Daniel's prophecy of the four-horned goat (Dan 8) |
| Ptolemy II Philadelphus | King of Egypt | Commissioned the Septuagint translation (~250 BC) according to the Letter of Aristeas; fostered the great Library of Alexandria |
| Antiochus IV Epiphanes | Seleucid king | Desecrated the Temple; forbidden Torah observance; the "abomination of desolation" (Dan 11:31; Matt 24:15); a type of the final Antichrist |
| Mattathias | Priest; father of the Maccabees | Sparked the revolt by refusing to sacrifice to idols; his five sons led the liberation |
| Judas Maccabeus | Military leader | "The Hammer"; recaptured Jerusalem; cleansed the Temple; instituted Hanukkah; died in battle 161 BC |
| Simon Maccabeus | High priest and ruler | Achieved full independence 142 BC; founded Hasmonean dynasty; assassinated by his son-in-law |
| Pompey the Great | Roman general | Captured Jerusalem 63 BC; entered the Holy of Holies (and left it untouched); ended Jewish independence |
| Hillel and Shammai | Pharisaic scholars | Active at the end of this period (~50 BC–10 AD); their schools of interpretation form the backdrop of Jesus's disputes with the Pharisees |
