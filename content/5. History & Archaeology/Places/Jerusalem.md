## Biblical Significance

Jerusalem stands at the center of the entire biblical narrative. David captured the Jebusite city of Zion and made it his capital (2 Samuel 5:6–9), bringing the Ark of the Covenant there and establishing it as the political and spiritual heart of Israel. His son Solomon built the First Temple on the threshing floor of Araunah (2 Chronicles 3:1), giving the worship of Yahweh a permanent home and fulfilling a promise God had been building toward since Sinai. The city's destruction by Nebuchadnezzar in 586 BC — the burning of the Temple, the deportation of the people — forms one of the Old Testament's most theologically weighty events, raising the sharpest questions about divine faithfulness. The return under Ezra and Nehemiah and the building of the Second Temple are equally foundational: Jerusalem is not merely geography, it is the meeting place of heaven and earth in Old Testament theology.

In the New Testament, Jerusalem is the destination of Jesus's life and ministry. His triumphal entry, cleansing of the Temple, Last Supper, arrest, crucifixion outside the city walls, and resurrection all take place in and around the city. The early church is born here at Pentecost (Acts 2), and Jerusalem remains the mother church and doctrinal reference point for the apostolic mission. Revelation's vision of the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21–22) recapitulates and transforms everything the historical city represents — the dwelling of God with humanity — making Jerusalem a theological bookend for the entire canon.

## Historical Background

Jerusalem's history stretches back at least to the Early Bronze Age (3rd millennium BC), though the ridge of the City of David shows occupation from the Chalcolithic period. The city is almost certainly the "Salem" whose king Melchizedek blessed Abram (Genesis 14:18), and it appears in Egyptian Execration Texts of the 19th–18th centuries BC as a recognized city-state. The Amarna Letters (~1350 BC) include six letters from a Canaanite king of Jerusalem named Abdi-Heba, who writes to Pharaoh Akhenaten warning of political instability and requesting Egyptian troops — providing clear extra-biblical attestation of the city before Israelite settlement.

The Jebusites controlled the site until David's conquest (~1000 BC), after which it became a royal Israelite city. Under Solomon, Hezekiah, Josiah, and the later kings, Jerusalem grew significantly. Hezekiah's massive construction projects — including the Siloam Tunnel and the Broad Wall — were defensive preparations against the Assyrian threat (701 BC). The city was destroyed twice by major powers: by Nebuchadnezzar (586 BC) and by Rome (70 AD). Under Herod the Great, the Second Temple platform was expanded into the largest manmade platform in the ancient world. The Roman emperor Hadrian rebuilt the city as Aelia Capitolina following the Bar Kokhba revolt (135 AD), suppressing its Jewish identity until the Byzantine period, when Christian emperors restored its religious centrality.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Charles Warren (1867–1870, Palestine Exploration Fund); Frederick Bliss and Archibald Dickie (1894–1897); Kathleen Kenyon (1961–1967); Benjamin Mazar (1968–1977, Temple Mount southern wall); Yigal Shiloh (1978–1985, City of David); Eilat Mazar (2005–2008, 2010–2013, City of David and Ophel); the Temple Mount Sifting Project (Gabriel Barkay and Zachi Dvira, 2004–present)

- **Large Stone Structure (Eilat Mazar, 2005):** A monumental Iron Age IIA public building on the summit of the City of David, constructed with boulders over 1 m across. Mazar identified it as David's palace based on pottery and its stratigraphic position beneath later Israelite fills; the identification remains debated but the structure's scale is unambiguous.
- **Stepped Stone Structure:** A massive Iron Age terraced retaining system on the eastern slope of the City of David, possibly the Millo of 2 Samuel 5:9; one of the largest Iron Age structures found in Israel.
- **Siloam Tunnel (Hezekiah's Tunnel):** Cut through bedrock ~533 m to bring water from the Gihon Spring to the Pool of Siloam inside the city walls (~701 BC). The Siloam Inscription — a 6-line Hebrew text describing the moment the two tunnel crews broke through — was found carved into the wall in 1880. The inscription was removed by Ottoman authorities and is now in the Istanbul Archaeological Museum.
- **Broad Wall:** An Israelite wall ~7 m thick discovered by Avigad in the Jewish Quarter, dated to Hezekiah (late 8th century BC), corresponding to Isaiah 22:10's description of houses torn down to reinforce the city's defenses before Sennacherib's invasion.
- **Burnt Room (Yigal Shiloh, 1984):** In Area G of the City of David excavations, a destruction layer with carbonized wood, arrowheads, and smashed pottery dated to 586 BC, providing direct physical evidence of Nebuchadnezzar's burning of the city.
- **Bulla of Gedeliah son of Pashhur (City of David):** A clay seal impression naming an official mentioned in Jeremiah 38:1 as an enemy of the prophet, found in the same burnt layer as other bullae from the late 7th/early 6th century BC. Now in the Israel Museum.
- **Pool of Siloam (2004):** Discovered during infrastructure repair work by the Israel Antiquities Authority. A large stepped pool with Herodian-era stonework, identified as the pool mentioned in John 9:7 where Jesus healed the blind man.
- **Herodian Street (Benjamin Mazar excavations, extended by later digs):** A broad paved street running along the western and southern walls of the Temple Mount, with shops beneath; a massive section of wall stones pushed onto the street from the Temple Mount parapet visible, consistent with the Roman destruction of 70 AD.
- **Southern Steps of the Temple Mount:** Broad monumental stairs ascending to the Huldah Gates, the main public entrance to the Temple complex; the steps themselves survive and show significant foot traffic in the Second Temple period — these are the likely steps where Jesus taught and Peter preached at Pentecost.
- **Temple Mount Sifting Project:** Over 500 tons of fill illegally removed from the Temple Mount by the Waqf in 1999 are being systematically sifted. Finds include First Temple period artifacts, Second Temple period coins, mosaic tesserae, and a gold medallion with a menorah from the early Byzantine period.
