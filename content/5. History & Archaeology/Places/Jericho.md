## Biblical Significance

Jericho is the first city Joshua and the Israelites encounter after crossing the Jordan River, making it the gateway to the Promised Land and the opening act of the conquest narrative. The account in Joshua 6 is deliberately miraculous: Israel marches around the city in silence for six days, seven priests blow ram's-horn trumpets, the people shout on the seventh day, and the walls collapse. The city is placed under the ban (herem) — total destruction consecrated to Yahweh — with only Rahab's household spared because she hid the Israelite spies (Joshua 2). Joshua pronounces a curse on anyone who would rebuild it (Joshua 6:26), a curse fulfilled centuries later in 1 Kings 16:34 when Hiel of Bethel loses his sons in its rebuilding. The fall of Jericho is thus a theological statement as much as a military account: victory belongs to Yahweh, not Israelite arms.

In the New Testament, Jericho reappears as the setting for two significant encounters of Jesus: his healing of blind Bartimaeus (and in Luke's account, two blind men) on the road out of the city (Mark 10:46–52; Luke 18:35–43), and his encounter with the tax collector Zacchaeus, who climbs a sycamore tree to see him (Luke 19:1–10). The road from Jericho to Jerusalem — ascending over 1,000 meters in roughly 27 km — provides the setting for the Parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37). Jericho was also associated with Elijah and Elisha, whose "school of prophets" was based there (2 Kings 2:4–5), and it was the last place Elijah visited before his translation to heaven.

## Historical Background

Tell es-Sultan is among the most ancient continuously inhabited sites on earth. Natufian hunter-gatherers established a camp near the perennial Ain es-Sultan spring around 10,000 BC, and by ~8500 BC a pre-pottery Neolithic settlement had emerged — one of the world's first towns — with a massive stone tower (the Jericho Tower, ~8.5 m tall and 9 m in diameter) and a perimeter wall, making it perhaps the oldest known walled settlement in human history. The site shows continuous occupation through the Chalcolithic, Early, Middle, and Late Bronze Ages, making it a palimpsest of Near Eastern urban history.

During the Middle Bronze Age (~1800–1550 BC), Jericho was a prosperous Canaanite city with impressive earthwork ramparts (a plastered glacis) and a thriving population evidenced by rich tomb groups containing pottery, scarabs, furniture, and organic remains preserved in remarkable condition. The Late Bronze Age occupation is significantly thinner — the question of what was or was not there at the time of the Israelite conquest (~1400 or ~1230 BC depending on chronological scheme) has been the central archaeological debate surrounding the historicity of the Joshua narrative. The Herodian winter palace complex at Tulul Abu el-'Alayiq (just west of Tell es-Sultan) testifies to Jericho's continued strategic and agricultural importance in the Roman period, as Herod the Great built three successive palaces there for winter retreat.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Charles Warren (1868, Palestine Exploration Fund, initial soundings); Ernst Sellin and Carl Watzinger (1907–1909, Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft); John Garstang (1930–1936, British School of Archaeology in Jerusalem); Kathleen Kenyon (1952–1958, British School of Archaeology / British Academy); Lorenzo Nigro and the Italian-Palestinian Expedition (2009–present); Ehud Netzer (Tulul Abu el-'Alayiq Herodian palace, 1973–2000)

- **Neolithic Tower (Kenyon excavations, Trench I):** A circular stone tower ~8.5 m tall built ~8500 BC, predating pottery and metal use, with an internal staircase of 22 steps. One of the most extraordinary architectural finds in Near Eastern archaeology. Now partly exposed in situ at the site.
- **Pre-Pottery Neolithic Skull Cache:** Plastered human skulls with shells set in the eye sockets, found by Kenyon in PPNB layers (~7000 BC); suggest an ancestor cult practice. Examples held at the Rockefeller Museum, Jerusalem, and the British Museum.
- **Middle Bronze Age Tombs (Garstang, then Kenyon):** Over 500 shaft tombs in the surrounding cemetery yielding wooden furniture (beds, tables, stools), pottery, scarabs, and personal items in extraordinary preservation due to the dry climate and tomb sealing. The organic material provides one of the most complete pictures of MB Canaanite material culture anywhere. Finds now distributed between the Rockefeller Museum and the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford.
- **City Wall Debate — Garstang's "City D" Walls:** Garstang identified a double mud-brick wall system (outer and inner walls) that had collapsed and burned, which he dated to the Late Bronze Age (~1400 BC) and associated with the Israelite conquest. He reported finding Egyptian scarabs up to the reign of Amenhotep III as the latest dateable artifacts beneath the destruction.
- **Kenyon's Stratigraphic Revision:** Kenyon's meticulous section-cutting in the 1950s demonstrated that Garstang's "City D" walls actually belonged to the Early Bronze Age (~2300 BC) and had been reused in the Middle Bronze Age. Her analysis found that the Late Bronze Age occupation (the period of the Israelite settlement) was represented by only a thin eroded layer, with no city wall and minimal architecture surviving. This finding — published in *Digging Up Jericho* (1957) and technical reports through the 1980s — became the basis for claims that Jericho was unoccupied at the time of the conquest. However, Kenyon herself noted significant erosion had likely removed evidence, and her LB strata are assessed by different scholars differently.
- **Bryant Wood's Reanalysis (1990):** Wood reexamined Kenyon's pottery from the destruction layer and argued the ceramics were consistent with a date of ~1400 BC (an "early" conquest date) rather than ~1550 BC as Kenyon had assumed; he also pointed to a storage jar full of grain as evidence the city fell in the spring (consistent with Joshua's timing) after only a brief siege. His conclusions remain debated.
- **Herodian Winter Palace (Netzer excavations):** Three successive palaces at Tulul Abu el-'Alayiq — the first two attributed to the Hasmonean period, the third a grand Herodian complex with a sunken garden (paradeisos), opus reticulatum (Roman concrete network facing), a large pool, and a bathhouse. These are among the best-preserved examples of Herodian architecture outside Jerusalem.
