# Caesarea Maritima

**Location:** Caesarea National Park, Israel — on the Mediterranean coast approximately 50 km north of Tel Aviv and 45 km south of Haifa; modern city of Or Akiva nearby; 32.5000° N, 34.8964° E
**Testament:** NT
**Key Themes:** [[Mission and Evangelism]] · [[Gentiles and the Gospel]] · [[Persecution and Suffering]] · [[Kingdom of God]]

---

## Biblical Significance

Caesarea Maritima is the Roman administrative capital of Judea during the New Testament period, and the book of Acts gives it more narrative real estate than almost any other city outside Jerusalem and Antioch. It first appears in Acts 8:40 when Philip the evangelist arrives after baptizing the Ethiopian eunuch on the road from Jerusalem, and Philip apparently settles there — Acts 21:8 refers to him still living in Caesarea years later when Paul visits, noting he has four daughters who prophesy. The city's most theologically pivotal moment comes in Acts 10, when the Roman centurion Cornelius — stationed at Caesarea — receives a vision directing him to send for Peter. Peter arrives, preaches the gospel, and the Holy Spirit falls on Cornelius's Gentile household before Peter even finishes speaking or administers baptism. This event is so significant that Peter recounts it twice more in Acts (11:1–18; 15:7–11), and it stands as the decisive narrative breakthrough of the gospel to the Gentiles — a world-historical turning point set in Herod the Great's Roman showcase city.

Paul passes through Caesarea multiple times in his missionary journeys (Acts 9:30; 18:22), but the city becomes the stage for the dramatic final act of his career. Transported from Jerusalem after an assassination plot, Paul is held under Roman custody at Caesarea for two years (Acts 23:35–24:27) — first under Felix, who hopes for a bribe, then under Festus. It is in Caesarea that Paul's legal drama unfolds: his defense before Felix (Acts 24), before Festus (Acts 25), and his masterful speech before King Agrippa II and his sister Bernice (Acts 26) — the longest recorded defense speech in Acts, culminating in Agrippa's famous remark: "In a short time would you persuade me to be a Christian?" (Acts 26:28). Paul's appeal to Caesar is accepted in Caesarea, and his voyage to Rome — and thus the final movement of Acts — begins here.

## Historical Background

Before Herod's construction, the site was a modest Phoenician anchorage called Strato's Tower, mentioned in sources from the 3rd–2nd centuries BC as a small harbor settlement. The Hasmonean dynasty controlled it briefly, and the site changed hands several times before Rome assigned it to Herod the Great in 30 BC. Herod transformed it between approximately 22 and 10 BC into one of the most ambitious engineering projects of the ancient world, naming it Caesarea Maritima in honor of Caesar Augustus. The project required constructing an entirely artificial deep-water harbor — called Sebastos (Greek for "Augustus") — in open Mediterranean waters with no natural protection. Josephus describes the construction in detail in *Antiquities* (XV.9.6) and *Jewish War* (I.21.5–8): enormous concrete blocks (hydraulic concrete made with pozzolanic volcanic ash mixed with seawater) were sunk as breakwaters, creating two massive curved moles enclosing a harbor larger than the Piraeus at Athens, with warehouses, towers, lighthouse, and a temple to Rome and Augustus visible from the sea.

Caesarea became the seat of the Roman governors of Judea (procurators, later proconsuls), including Pontius Pilate, who maintained his primary residence here rather than in Jerusalem. The city was a mixed population of Jews, Samaritans, Greeks, Romans, and Syrians, and ethnic tensions between Jewish and Gentile populations were a recurring source of violence — the riots in Caesarea in 66 AD were among the precipitating events of the First Jewish Revolt. The city remained an important administrative and commercial center through the Byzantine period, when it became the seat of an important Christian bishop and center of theological scholarship (Origen founded his famous library here in the early 3rd century; Eusebius of Caesarea, the church historian, was bishop here ~313–339 AD). The Arab conquest in 641 AD and the subsequent Crusader occupation (1101–1265 AD) added further architectural layers before the city's eventual abandonment and burial.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Antonio Frova (Italian Mission, 1959–1964, discovered the Pilate Stone); the Joint Expedition to Caesarea Maritima (JECM), a consortium of American universities led by Robert Bull (1971–1984); the Caesarea Ancient Harbor Excavation Project (CAHEP) with underwater archaeology by Robert Hohlfelder and Avner Raban (1980s–1990s); the Caesarea Maritima Excavations directed by Kenneth Holum (University of Maryland, 1989–2010s); Israel Antiquities Authority ongoing salvage excavations

- **The Pilate Stone (Antonio Frova, 1961):** During Italian excavations of the Caesarea theater, a limestone block (82 × 68 cm) was found reused as a step in a staircase of a later phase of the theater. Its inscription, in Latin, reads (reconstructed): "[...] TIBERIEUM / [PON]TIUS PILATUS / [PRAEF]ECTUS IUDA[EA]E / [FECIT D]E[DICAVIT]" — "Pontius Pilatus, Prefect of Judaea, built and dedicated [the Tiberieum (a building in honor of Tiberius)]." This is the only inscription ever found bearing Pilate's name, and at the time of its discovery was the first — and remains the primary — physical archaeological evidence that Pontius Pilate was a historical figure and held the title "Prefect" rather than "Procurator" (as some ancient sources inaccurately state). The original stone is held at the Israel Museum, Jerusalem; a replica stands in situ at the theater.
- **The Sebastos Harbor (Underwater Excavations, 1980s–1990s):** Underwater surveys by Raban and Hohlfelder, using side-scan sonar, sub-bottom profiling, and diving surveys, mapped the full extent of the ancient harbor breakwaters buried beneath the Mediterranean seabed. The survey confirmed Josephus's dimensions and revealed the harbor's hydraulic concrete construction — among the earliest large-scale uses of pozzolanic Roman concrete in the eastern Mediterranean. The sunken concrete blocks showed evidence of earthquake damage (likely the 130 AD earthquake) that caused much of the harbor to sink and ultimately led to the facility's decline.
- **Herodian Temple Platform:** On the harbor promontory, the podium of Herod's temple to Rome and Augustus (the Augusteum) has been identified; the platform measures ~46 × 25 m. Literary sources describe the temple as containing colossal statues of Augustus (compared to the Zeus at Olympia) and Rome (comparable to Hera at Argos). No statuary has survived.
- **Roman Theater:** One of the earliest Roman theaters in the eastern Mediterranean, built by Herod and capable of seating ~3,500 spectators. The Pilate Stone was found here. The theater has been extensively excavated and partially restored; it is still used for concerts today. Excavations revealed multiple phases of use and renovation, including Byzantine-period reuse.
- **Hippodrome/Stadium:** An elongated stadium ~450 m long running south of the harbor, capable of holding ~10,000–15,000 spectators for chariot races and athletic events, one of the longest such structures in the Roman east. Excavations have confirmed the starting gates (carceres), the central spine (spina), and seating arrangements.
- **Roman Aqueducts:** Two aqueducts (a High Level Aqueduct and a Low Level Aqueduct) brought water to the city from springs on Mount Carmel, ~15 km to the north. The High Level Aqueduct, built by Herod and later extended by the Roman legions (Latin inscriptions from Legio II Traiana Fortis and Legio VI Ferrata are preserved on the arcade), is one of the best-preserved Roman aqueducts in the Levant, with extensive sections still standing.
- **Mithraeum:** A well-preserved Mithraic temple found within the Crusader city, one of the few excavated Mithraea in Israel, with a characteristic barrel-vaulted chamber and evidence of ritual use — consistent with the Roman military presence (Mithraism was popular among soldiers).
- **Byzantine Street and Governor's Palace:** A colonnaded Byzantine street (cardo) and what has been identified as the praetorium (governor's palace) — relevant to Acts 23:35, which describes Paul being kept in "Herod's praetorium" — have been partially excavated on the seaside promontory. The promontory palace shows lavish mosaic floors and a freshwater pool.
- **Crusader City Walls and Cathedral:** The Crusader city of Caesarea, built largely on top of the Roman and Byzantine remains, left substantial walls, moat, and the Cathedral of St. Peter (converting the earlier mosque). These remains are visible throughout the national park.
