# Babylon

**Location:** Tell Babil and surrounding ruins, ~85 km south of Baghdad, Babil Governorate, Iraq — adjacent to modern Hillah city; GPS ~32.54°N 44.42°E
**Testament:** Both
**Key Themes:** [[Exile and Return]] · [[Judgment and Restoration]] · [[Sovereignty of God]]

---

## Biblical Significance

Babylon enters the biblical story early — as the site of the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:1–9), where humanity's unified rebellion against God results in the scattering of nations and the confusion of languages. But Babylon's overwhelming biblical weight falls on the Exile period. In 605 BC Nebuchadnezzar II began deporting Judean nobles to Babylon; in 597 BC he took King Jehoiachin and ten thousand skilled craftsmen and soldiers; and in 586 BC he destroyed Jerusalem, burned the temple, and deported the remaining population. This catastrophe — the Babylonian exile — is the hinge event of the Old Testament. It raised the most urgent theological question Israel had ever faced: had God abandoned his people? The entire prophetic corpus of Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and much of Isaiah responds to this crisis. Ezekiel prophesied to the exiles from the banks of the Chebar Canal in Babylonia (Ezek 1:1–3), and his visions of the divine chariot (*merkavah*) and the restored temple address a people who had lost both temple and land.

Daniel's entire ministry unfolds in Babylon across the reigns of Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar, Darius the Mede, and Cyrus the Persian. Daniel and his companions navigate the demands of the empire while maintaining covenant faithfulness — refusing the king's food, surviving the furnace, reading the handwriting on the wall, enduring the lion's den. These narratives became paradigmatic for Jewish and Christian communities living under powerful pagan empires. In the New Testament, Babylon becomes a theological symbol: in 1 Peter 5:13 it is almost certainly a code name for Rome, and in Revelation 17–18 "Babylon the Great" is the archetypal city of worldly power, luxury, and opposition to God — whose fall is celebrated as the vindication of martyred saints.

## Historical Background

Babylon was one of the great cities of the ancient world for over two thousand years. It rose to prominence under the Amorite king Hammurabi (c. 1792–1750 BC), who unified Mesopotamia and promulgated the famous law code bearing his name — 282 laws inscribed on a 2.25-meter black diorite stele, now in the Louvre, Paris. Babylon's Old Babylonian period saw it become the cultural and religious center of Mesopotamia. After periods of Kassite, Assyrian, and other domination, it rose to supreme power again under the Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) dynasty. Under Nabopolassar (626–605 BC) and especially his son Nebuchadnezzar II (605–562 BC), Babylon was rebuilt on a scale unmatched in the ancient world.

At its height, Babylon covered approximately 850 hectares and may have housed 200,000 or more people. The city was defended by a double circuit of walls — the outer wall broad enough, according to Herodotus, for two chariots to pass side by side on top. The Ishtar Gate, the eighth gate of the inner city wall, stood at the northern entrance and was faced with deep blue glazed brick decorated with alternating rows of aurochs (wild bulls) and *sirrush* (dragon-like creatures sacred to Marduk). The Processional Street leading through the gate was lined with lions in relief. The Etemenanki ziggurat — "House of the Foundation of Heaven and Earth" — dedicated to Marduk, rose in seven stages to a height possibly exceeding 90 meters and is the most plausible physical candidate for the Tower of Babel tradition. The Hanging Gardens, if they existed in Babylon rather than Nineveh, were one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. Babylon fell to Cyrus the Great of Persia in 539 BC, who allowed the Jewish exiles to return to their homeland — a decree preserved in the Cyrus Cylinder (British Museum).

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Robert Koldewey, Deutsche Orient-Gesellschaft (1899–1917); Hormuzd Rassam (1879–1882, earlier British excavation); Iraqi State Board of Antiquities and Heritage (various); Saddam Hussein's controversial reconstruction program (1980s–1990s)

- **Koldewey's excavations (1899–1917):** The most systematic scientific excavation of Babylon to date. Koldewey identified and excavated the Ishtar Gate and Processional Street, Nebuchadnezzar's Southern Palace (the main royal complex covering approximately 322 x 190 meters with five large courtyards), the Northern Citadel, the Esagila temple complex of Marduk, and the outline of the Etemenanki ziggurat. He pioneered the use of decauville railway tracks to remove debris and employed a workforce of up to 200 laborers daily. His field records remain the primary stratigraphic documentation of the site.
- **Ishtar Gate (excavated and reconstructed):** The original glazed brickwork was excavated, shipped to Berlin, and reconstructed in the Pergamon Museum, where it stands today as one of the most spectacular exhibits of ancient Near Eastern art. The gate standing currently at the Babylon site is Saddam Hussein's modern reconstruction. The Pergamon Museum's gate represents the lower portion; the upper, more ornate section remained in situ or was lost.
- **Jehoiachin's Ration Tablets:** Among the most significant biblical confirmations from Babylon. Koldewey's excavations of the vaulted storerooms (the "Southern Building" near the Ishtar Gate) yielded cuneiform administrative tablets — later published by Ernst Weidner in 1939 — listing rations of oil and barley distributed to "Ia-u-kin, king of the land of Ia-ku-du" (Jehoiachin, king of Judah) and his five sons. The tablets date to between 595 and 570 BC and directly corroborate 2 Kings 25:27–30, which records that Evil-merodach (Amel-Marduk) released Jehoiachin from prison and gave him a regular allowance. The tablets are housed in the Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin.
- **Nebuchadnezzar's Palace (Stratum of Neo-Babylonian period):** Koldewey excavated the throne room of the Southern Palace (Throne Room B), with walls originally faced with deep blue glazed brick and yellow, white, and blue lion reliefs. The throne room measured approximately 52 x 17 meters. Inscriptions on bricks throughout the palace bear Nebuchadnezzar II's standard stamp in Akkadian.
- **Cyrus Cylinder (found 1879, Rassam excavation of Esagila):** A clay barrel-cylinder inscribed in Akkadian in which Cyrus the Great claims that Marduk chose him to liberate Babylon and describes allowing deported peoples to return to their homelands and restore their temples. While not naming Israel specifically, this is the imperial policy reflected in Ezra 1:1–4 and Isaiah 44:28–45:1. Now in the British Museum (BM 90920).
- **Saddam Hussein's Reconstruction (1980s–1990s):** Highly controversial — Saddam ordered the reconstruction of Nebuchadnezzar's palace and outer walls using new bricks stamped with his own name alongside Nebuchadnezzar's ("In the era of Saddam Hussein, protector of Iraq..."). This has caused significant damage to the original archaeological layers beneath and complicated future stratigraphic work.
