# Qumran & the Dead Sea Scrolls

**Location:** Khirbet Qumran, northwestern shore of the Dead Sea, West Bank — approximately 1.5 km from the shoreline, 13 km south of Jericho; GPS ~31.74°N 35.46°E
**Testament:** Intertestamental / OT Background
**Key Themes:** [[Scripture and Canon]] · [[Messianic Expectation]] · [[Covenant Community]]

---

## Biblical Significance

The Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered in eleven caves near Khirbet Qumran between 1947 and 1956, constitute the single most significant manuscript discovery in the history of biblical scholarship. The collection includes fragments or complete copies of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther, predating the previously oldest known Hebrew manuscripts (the Aleppo Codex, c. 920 AD, and the Leningrad Codex, c. 1008 AD) by roughly one thousand years. The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a), found intact in Cave 1 in 1947, runs the full 7.34 meters of Isaiah's 66 chapters and is virtually identical to the Masoretic Text — a stunning confirmation of textual transmission accuracy across a millennium. Isaiah 53, the Suffering Servant passage central to New Testament Christology, appears in full and without significant deviation from later manuscripts.

Beyond confirming textual accuracy, the scrolls illuminate the world of late Second Temple Judaism into which Jesus and the New Testament writers were born. Sectarian texts like the Community Rule (1QS), the War Scroll (1QM), and the Thanksgiving Hymns (1QH) reveal a community that practiced communal living, ritual purity baths (*miqva'ot*), shared meals, and intense scriptural study — practices that bear striking parallels to early Christian community life. The messianic texts found at Qumran, including 4Q521 ("A Messianic Apocalypse") which describes a coming messiah who heals the blind, raises the dead, and preaches good news to the poor, provide direct background to Jesus's answer to John the Baptist in Matthew 11:4–5. The scrolls demonstrate that Jewish messianic expectation in the first century was varied, active, and scripturally rooted.

## Historical Background

Khirbet Qumran was first occupied in the Iron Age II period (approximately 8th–7th centuries BC), possibly as an Israelite fortress. It was then abandoned and reoccupied circa 150–100 BC, most likely by a sectarian Jewish group now broadly identified as Essenes — a sect described by the Jewish historian Josephus (*Jewish War* 2.8.2–13), the philosopher Philo (*Every Good Man Is Free* 75–91), and the Roman geographer Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 5.17.4), who places them near the Dead Sea. The Essenes rejected the Jerusalem temple establishment (regarding the Hasmonean high priesthood as illegitimate), withdrew into the desert, and dedicated themselves to Torah observance, ritual purity, and the expectation of an imminent eschatological war.

The community flourished through several phases. Period Ib (c. 100–31 BC) saw major construction of the site's central complex — a scriptorium, a large assembly hall, cisterns, and ritual baths (*miqva'ot*). An earthquake in 31 BC damaged the site and it was briefly abandoned, then rebuilt (Period II, c. 4 BC–68 AD). When the Roman Tenth Legion (Legio X Fretensis) advanced into the region during the First Jewish-Roman War (66–73 AD), the community hid their library in the surrounding caves and the site was destroyed. Some scrolls show signs of hasty wrapping in linen and placement in ceramic jars specifically designed to preserve them — a deliberate act of preservation, not permanent storage.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** Roland de Vaux OP and G. Lankester Harding, École Biblique et Archéologique Française / Jordan Department of Antiquities (1951–1956); Yizhar Hirschfeld, Hebrew University (1996–2004, alternative interpretation); Randall Price and Oren Gutfeld, subsequent cave surveys

- **Cave 1 (1947 / excavated 1949):** The original discovery cave, explored by Bedouin shepherds of the Ta'amireh tribe in late 1946 or early 1947. Contents included seven major scrolls in ceramic jars: the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a), a second partial Isaiah scroll (1QIsa^b), the Community Rule (1QS), the Habakkuk Pesher (1QpHab — a line-by-line commentary on Habakkuk), the Hodayot/Thanksgiving Hymns (1QH), the War Scroll (1QM), and the Genesis Apocryphon (1QapGen). All are now housed at the Shrine of the Book, Israel Museum, Jerusalem.
- **Cave 4 (discovered 1952):** The richest cave, cut directly into the marl terrace adjacent to the settlement ruins. Contained approximately 15,000 fragments representing around 575 manuscripts. Finds included multiple copies of Deuteronomy, Psalms, and Isaiah; the 4QMMT ("Some Works of the Torah," a legal letter possibly from the Teacher of Righteousness); 4Q521 (messianic text); and the earliest known fragments of the Psalms scroll. Fragments distributed to international scholarly teams; now housed primarily at the Israel Antiquities Authority, Rockefeller Museum, Jerusalem.
- **Cave 3 (1952):** Contained the Copper Scroll (3Q15) — a unique document engraved on rolled copper sheets listing 64 locations of hidden treasures (gold, silver, temple vessels). The treasures have never been found; the scroll is housed at the Jordan Museum, Amman.
- **Cave 11 (1956):** The last major cave excavated. Yielded the Temple Scroll (11QT), the longest of all the Dead Sea Scrolls at 8.148 meters, containing a version of Mosaic law and an idealized temple blueprint. Also yielded the Great Psalms Scroll (11QPsa), which includes Psalm 151 (known from the Greek Septuagint) and several previously unknown compositions. Temple Scroll now in the Shrine of the Book, Israel Museum.
- **Qumran Settlement (de Vaux, 1951–1956):** De Vaux identified a scriptorium (Locus 30) containing inkwells and plastered writing benches, confirming the site's role in manuscript production. The complex included two stories of rooms, large communal dining halls (Locus 77), over 10 *miqva'ot* (ritual immersion pools), and a large cemetery of approximately 1,200 graves, predominantly adult males — consistent with a celibate or semi-celibate sectarian community.

## Key Discoveries

| Discovery | Date Found | Significance |
|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a), Cave 1 | 1947 (Bedouin); formally acquired 1948–1954 | Complete text of Isaiah ~125 BC; oldest biblical manuscript by 1,000 years; confirms Masoretic accuracy; includes Isaiah 53 in full |
| Community Rule (1QS), Cave 1 | 1947 | Describes the Essene community's laws, initiation, communal meals, and eschatological beliefs; parallels to early Christian community life |
| Habakkuk Pesher (1QpHab), Cave 1 | 1947 | First known example of *pesher* (line-by-line prophetic commentary applied to the sect's own history); method parallels NT use of OT |
| Copper Scroll (3Q15), Cave 3 | 1952 | Lists 64 locations of buried treasure totaling 4,600 talents of gold and silver; possibly connected to Second Temple treasury; Jordan Museum, Amman |
| 4Q521 "Messianic Apocalypse," Cave 4 | 1952 (fragments) | Describes the messiah healing blind, raising dead, preaching good news to poor — near-verbatim to Jesus's words in Matt 11:4–5 |
| Temple Scroll (11QT), Cave 11 | 1956 | Longest scroll (8.148 m); rewritten Torah and idealized temple dimensions; Shrine of the Book, Israel Museum |
| Inkwells and writing benches, Locus 30 | 1953 (de Vaux) | Confirms on-site manuscript copying; identified as scriptorium |
