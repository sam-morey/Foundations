# Corinth

**Location:** Ancient Corinth archaeological site, Corinthia regional unit, Peloponnese, Greece — approximately 5 km southwest of modern Corinth city, 78 km west-southwest of Athens; GPS ~37.91°N 22.88°E
**Testament:** NT
**Key Themes:** [[The Church]] · [[Grace and Law]] · [[Resurrection]]

---

## Biblical Significance

Corinth is one of the most important cities in the story of earliest Christianity. Paul arrived here on his second missionary journey (c. 50–51 AD), having come through Athens where he had limited success (Acts 17). In Corinth he stayed for eighteen months — his longest stay in any city during his missionary journeys to that point (Acts 18:11). He worked as a tentmaker alongside Aquila and Priscilla, who had been expelled from Rome under Claudius's edict. He preached in the synagogue, then (after Jewish resistance) moved next door to the house of Titus Justus. The ruler of the synagogue, Crispus, believed — along with many Corinthians (Acts 18:8). Paul received a vision in the night: "Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you" (Acts 18:9–10). Eventually he was dragged before the proconsul Gallio, who dismissed the charges as an internal Jewish religious dispute — a ruling of great importance for the early church's legal status.

Paul wrote at least four letters to the Corinthian church (two survive as 1 and 2 Corinthians). The city's specific character directly shapes the problems he addresses: factions rallying around different teachers (Paul, Apollos, Cephas) likely reflect the Corinthian culture of competitive rhetoric and status display; the sexual immorality tolerated in the congregation (1 Cor 5–6) reflects the city's infamous reputation; the abuse of the Lord's Supper (1 Cor 11) along class lines reflects the profound economic stratification of Roman colonial society; the obsession with tongues and spiritual gifts (1 Cor 12–14) reflects a culture that prized visible demonstrations of power and status. Paul's response to each — calling for cruciform love (1 Cor 13), a body with many parts (1 Cor 12), and the magnificent theology of resurrection as the ground of everything (1 Cor 15) — makes 1 Corinthians perhaps the most practically comprehensive of all his letters.

## Historical Background

Corinth's location made it one of the most strategically valuable sites in the ancient Mediterranean world. The city sat at the narrow neck of the isthmus connecting mainland Greece to the Peloponnese peninsula, flanked by two ports: Lechaion on the Corinthian Gulf (facing west toward Italy and Rome) and Cenchreae on the Saronic Gulf (facing east toward Asia Minor and the Levant). Ships trading between the Adriatic and the Aegean could avoid the dangerous 300-km sail around the Peloponnese by transporting their cargoes (and sometimes their ships, dragged on rollers) across the roughly 6-km *diolkos* portage road — a practice documented from at least the 6th century BC. This made Corinth fabulously wealthy and cosmopolitan.

The Greek city of Corinth was destroyed by the Roman general Lucius Mummius in 146 BC, its population enslaved or killed, and its art treasures shipped to Rome. The site sat largely uninhabited for a century. Julius Caesar refounded it as a Roman colony, *Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis*, in 44 BC — the same year of his assassination — and it was rapidly settled by freedmen, veterans, and merchants. By Paul's time it was the capital of the Roman province of Achaia, its population a diverse mix of Romans, Greeks, Jews, and people from across the eastern Mediterranean. The city had re-acquired a reputation for sexual license; the phrase *Korinthiazesthai* ("to Corinthianize") was a byword in the ancient world for sexual immorality, and the temple of Aphrodite on the Acrocorinth hill above the city was famous for its cult prostitution — though the extent of this practice in Roman Corinth (as opposed to earlier Greek Corinth) is debated by scholars.

## Archaeological Excavations

**Key Excavators:** American School of Classical Studies at Athens (ASCSA), continuous excavations from 1896 to the present — the longest continuously operating excavation in the history of classical archaeology

- **The Bema / Judgment Seat (excavated 1930s–1950s):** The *bema* was the raised stone platform in the center of the Corinthian *agora* (forum) from which Roman magistrates delivered judgments and official proclamations. It was faced with white and blue marble, with two small waiting rooms at either end. Excavators identified this structure as the location where Paul was brought before the proconsul Gallio (Acts 18:12–17). The *bema* dates to the mid-1st century AD and corresponds precisely to the period of Paul's visit. This is one of the most concrete physical connections between a named biblical event and an excavated structure.
- **Gallio Inscription (found at Delphi, 1905; published 1910):** A fragmentary inscription from the sanctuary at Delphi, found in nine pieces, records Emperor Claudius's letter acknowledging Lucius Junius Gallio as proconsul of Achaia. By cross-referencing the emperor's titles in the inscription with known dates of Claudius's tribunician powers, scholars date Gallio's proconsulship to approximately 51–52 AD — giving Paul's Corinthian ministry one of the most precisely dated anchors in early Christian chronology. The inscription is in the Delphi Archaeological Museum.
- **"Erastus" Pavement Inscription (found 1929):** Excavations near the northeast corner of the theater uncovered a large limestone paving block inscribed in Latin: "ERASTVS PRO AEDILIT[ATE] S[UA] P[ECUNIA] STRAVIT" — "Erastus, in return for his aedileship, laid [this pavement] at his own expense." Paul mentions Erastus as "the city treasurer" who sends greetings from Corinth in Romans 16:23, and in Acts 19:22 he is one of Paul's co-workers. While certainty is impossible, the inscription is dated to the 1st century AD, the name is uncommon, and the detail of a civic official connected to Corinth matches precisely. The inscription is still *in situ* at the site.
- **Synagogue Lintel Inscription (found 1898):** A white marble lintel fragment bearing the partial Greek inscription "SYNAGOGE HEBRAIO[N]" — "Synagogue of the Hebrews." Though found in a late context (built into a later stairway), it likely came from a first-century synagogue building in the city. It is housed in the Corinth Archaeological Museum and may relate to the synagogue where Paul preached (Acts 18:4).
- **"Meat Market" (Macellum) Inscription (found 1898):** A marble block inscribed with the Latin word "MACELLVM" (*macellum* = meat/food market) confirms the existence of the commercial market Paul references in 1 Corinthians 10:25: "Eat whatever is sold in the meat market (*makellon*) without raising any question on the ground of conscience." The word *makellon* in Paul is a direct Greek transliteration of the Latin *macellum*.
- **Temple of Apollo (partially excavated, standing):** Seven Doric columns from an archaic Temple of Apollo (c. 540 BC) still stand above the forum level — among the oldest standing Greek columns in the world. The temple was visible in Paul's time, though it was already ancient. Paul's discussion of food offered to idols (1 Cor 8–10) was directly relevant in a city where temples were embedded in the commercial and social life of the community.
- **Theater (excavated 1920s–1930s):** The large theater, capacity approximately 15,000–18,000, was in use in the Roman period. Inscriptions name gladiatorial games and theatrical performances. The theater, combined with the odeum nearby, formed a major entertainment complex consistent with Paul's oblique references to athletic competitions (1 Cor 9:24–27: "Do you not know that in a race all the runners run, but only one receives the prize?").
