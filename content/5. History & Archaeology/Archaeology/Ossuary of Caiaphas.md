**Type:** Limestone ossuary (bone box) with Aramaic inscription
**Date:** 1st century AD (ossuary style and inscription consistent with Second Temple period)
**Discovered:** 1990, Jerusalem — accidentally uncovered during construction work in the Peace Forest / North Talpiot area, south of the Old City
**Current Location:** Israel Museum, Jerusalem
**Biblical Connection:** The trial of Jesus; the Passion narrative; Matthew 26, John 11 and 18
**Key Themes:** [[Sacrifice & Atonement]] · [[Messiah & Christ]] · [[Judgment]]

---

## The Discovery

In November 1990, construction workers excavating in Jerusalem's Peace Forest (also called North Talpiot) inadvertently broke through the ceiling of a burial cave cut into the rock. Israeli archaeologists from the Israel Antiquities Authority, led by Zvi Greenhut, were quickly called in and conducted an emergency excavation of the tomb before further damage occurred.

The cave contained a burial chamber with twelve limestone ossuaries — the bone boxes used in Jewish burial practice during the Second Temple period (1st century BC – 70 AD). In this custom, the body was first laid in a niche in the cave (a *loculus*) for a year; once the flesh had decomposed, the bones were gathered and placed in a limestone box (an ossuary), which was then stored permanently in the family tomb. The most elaborate ossuary in the cave — one of two that bore inscriptions — was decorated with a carved floral rosette on the side and bore an Aramaic inscription identifying its occupant.

---

## What It Says

The ossuary bears two inscriptions, both in Aramaic — one on the side and one on the back, both identifying the same person:

**Side inscription:** יהוסף בר קפא (*Yehosef bar Qafa* — "Joseph son of Caiaphas")

**Back inscription (cruder lettering, possibly added later):** יהוסף בר קיפא (*Yehosef bar Qaifa*)

The ossuary contained the bones of six individuals: two infants, a toddler, a teenage boy, an adult woman, and a male approximately 60 years of age. The bones of the 60-year-old male are believed to be those of the tomb's primary occupant — Joseph son of Caiaphas.

The name "Caiaphas" (*Qayafa* / *Qaifa*) is the Aramaic family name. The given name, common in the period, was "Joseph." The Jewish historian Josephus (*Jewish Antiquities* 18.2.2; 18.4.3) specifically names the high priest as "Joseph who was called Caiaphas" — Joseph Caiaphas. This matches the ossuary inscription precisely: "Joseph son of Caiaphas" — where "Caiaphas" functions as a family/clan name by which Joseph was commonly known.

---

## Biblical Significance

Caiaphas served as high priest from approximately 18–36 AD — the longest tenure of any high priest in the Second Temple period, and precisely overlapping with the ministries of John the Baptist and Jesus. His appointment and longevity were due in part to his relationship with the Roman prefect — he was appointed by the procurator Valerius Gratus and served through Pontius Pilate's entire tenure, removed only when Pilate himself was recalled.

The New Testament references to Caiaphas are concentrated in the Passion narratives:

- In John 11, following the raising of Lazarus, Caiaphas convenes the Sanhedrin and delivers the decisive ruling: "It is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 11|John 11:50]]). John specifically notes that this was unwitting prophecy — "being high priest that year he prophesied" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 11|John 11:51]]).
- In Matthew 26, Caiaphas is the high priest who convenes the Sanhedrin at his palace, questions Jesus under oath about his identity, and pronounces the charge of blasphemy when Jesus affirms his divine sonship: "He has uttered blasphemy. What further witnesses do we need?" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/40. Matthew/Matthew 26|Matt 26:65]]).
- In John 18, Caiaphas conducts the preliminary interrogation of Jesus before sending him to Pontius Pilate for Roman trial and execution.

The ossuary connects this named historical figure — the man who formally declared Jesus guilty of blasphemy and handed him to the Romans for crucifixion — to a physical artifact. The bones of a man approximately 60 years old in the ossuary are consistent with the Caiaphas of the Gospels.

The find is significant for multiple reasons:

**1. Personal connection to the Passion:** Caiaphas is one of the most consequential figures in the crucifixion narrative. His ossuary is one of the most direct tangible connections between a named New Testament figure and a surviving physical artifact — rivaled only by the Pilate Stone.

**2. Confirmation of Jewish burial practice:** The ossuary confirms the secondary burial practice that Jesus's burial in a tomb (before resurrection) reflects — the same culture in which ossuaries were used.

**3. Josephus's corroboration:** The combination of the New Testament, Josephus, and the ossuary inscription creates a three-way convergence on the same individual — Joseph son of Caiaphas, known as "Caiaphas," who served as high priest in the period of Jesus's trial.

**4. The bone boxes of the very courtroom:** The cave contained the bones not only of Caiaphas but of his family — members of the ruling priestly class whose decisions shaped the events of Passion week. These are not abstract historical figures; they left their bones.

## Scholarly Discussion

The identification of the ossuary as belonging to the high priest Caiaphas of the Gospels and Josephus is accepted by most scholars but not without caveats:

- **"Caiaphas" as a common name:** Some scholars have cautioned that "Caiaphas" or "Qafa" might have been a more common clan name than assumed, and that multiple men named "Joseph son of Caiaphas" might have existed in first-century Jerusalem. However, the combination of the name, the location (a elite tomb appropriate for a high-priestly family), the date, and the convergence with Josephus's specific wording makes the identification highly probable.
- **The ornate ossuary:** The elaborately decorated ossuary with its carved rosettes is consistent with the wealth and status of a high-priestly family.
- **Subsequent handling:** In 2011, a second ossuary inscribed "Miriam daughter of Yeshua son of Caiaphas, priests of Ma'aziah from Beth 'Imri" was identified, providing additional epigraphic evidence of the Caiaphas priestly clan in the same period.

The original bones were reinterred by religious authorities shortly after the archaeological study was completed; the empty ossuary is displayed in the Israel Museum.
