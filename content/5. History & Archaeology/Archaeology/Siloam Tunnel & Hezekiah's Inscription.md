**Type:** Rock-cut tunnel (533 m) + limestone wall inscription (6 lines, ancient Hebrew)
**Date:** ~701 BC (reign of King Hezekiah of Judah)
**Discovered:** Tunnel explored by Edward Robinson (1838); Siloam Inscription discovered 1880 by a local boy exploring the tunnel
**Current Location:** Tunnel in situ beneath the City of David, Jerusalem (open to visitors); Siloam Inscription at the Istanbul Archaeological Museum (removed in 1890 during Ottoman rule of Palestine)
**Biblical Connection:** 2 Kings 20:20; 2 Chronicles 32:2–4, 30; Isaiah 22:11; John 9:7 (Pool of Siloam)
**Key Themes:** [[Sovereignty of God]] · [[Faith & Trust]] · [[Prophecy & Fulfillment]]

---

## The Discovery

In 1838, American biblical scholar and geographer Edward Robinson explored the water tunnel running beneath the City of David in Jerusalem, noting its remarkable engineering. The tunnel's purpose and approximate date were understood — it was clearly connected to the Gihon Spring — but its full extent and ancient inscription had not yet been found.

In 1880, a boy playing in the Siloam Pool at the southern end of the tunnel reportedly waded in and discovered carved letters on the tunnel wall near the southern opening. The inscription was brought to the attention of scholars; a young German student named Conrad Schick documented and published it. The inscription, carved in classical Biblical Hebrew in an elegant lapidary script, proved to be from the Iron Age II period (8th–7th century BC) — the era of King Hezekiah. In 1890, during the Ottoman period, the inscription was removed from the tunnel wall and sent to Constantinople (Istanbul), where it remains in the Istanbul Archaeological Museum. The empty socket where the inscription panel was cut from the wall is still visible in the tunnel today.

Carbon-14 dating of organic material found in the tunnel plaster has since confirmed a date in the 8th century BC, consistent with Hezekiah's reign (~715–687 BC) and specifically with the period of Sennacherib's threat (701 BC).

---

## What It Says

The six-line Siloam Inscription reads (in translation from the Hebrew):

> "[...the completion of] the tunnel. And this was the account of the breakthrough. While [the tunnelers were still] wielding the axe, each toward his fellow, and while there were still three cubits to be broken through, [there was heard] the voice of a man calling to his fellow, for there was a crack in the rock on the right and on the left. And on the day of the breakthrough, the tunnelers struck, each toward his fellow, axe against axe. And the water flowed from the spring toward the pool, one thousand and two hundred cubits, and one hundred cubits was the height of the rock above the head of the tunnelers."

This is a first-person narrative — carved by or for the workers who completed the tunnel — describing the dramatic moment when two digging teams, working from opposite ends, met in the middle. They heard each other's voices through the rock, adjusted their course to converge, and broke through. The tunnel is 533 meters long (consistent with "1,200 cubits"), and the height of the rock above varies from 22 to 40 meters.

## Biblical Significance

The Siloam Tunnel and its inscription illuminate several biblical texts simultaneously:

**Hezekiah's water preparation (2 Kings 20:20):** The summary verse for Hezekiah's reign mentions explicitly: "The rest of the deeds of Hezekiah and all his might and how he made the pool and the conduit and brought water into the city, are they not written in the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Judah?" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/12. II Kings/II Kings 20|2 Kings 20:20]]). This is not elaborated, but the Siloam Tunnel is the only known pool-and-conduit water engineering project from Hezekiah's reign, making the identification certain. Second Chronicles 32:2–4 and 32:30 add: "He planned with his officers and his mighty men to stop the water of the springs that were outside the city; and they helped him. A great many people were gathered, and they stopped all the springs and the brook that flowed through the land, saying, 'Why should the kings of Assyria come and find much water?'"

This is the strategic context: Sennacherib's army is approaching (701 BC). Hezekiah cuts off the external water supply and redirects it underground into the city, preparing Jerusalem for siege. The Siloam Tunnel is not merely an engineering marvel — it is a desperate act of faith under threat, channeling water from the Gihon Spring (which would otherwise be accessible to Assyrian forces besieging the city) into the protected interior Pool of Siloam.

**Isaiah's rebuke (Isaiah 22:11):** Isaiah delivers a complex oracle about Jerusalem's defensive preparations and rebukes the city for trusting in its own engineering: "You made a reservoir between the two walls for the water of the old pool. But you did not look to him who did it, or see him who planned it long ago" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/23. Isaiah/Isaiah 22|Isa 22:11]]). The Siloam Tunnel may be the specific structure Isaiah references — a "reservoir between the two walls" for water. If so, the inscription and Isaiah's oracle describe the same engineering project from two different theological perspectives.

**The Pool of Siloam and Jesus's miracle (John 9:7):** The pool at the southern end of Hezekiah's tunnel — the Pool of Siloam — is where Jesus sends the man born blind to wash after applying mud to his eyes: "Go, wash in the pool of Siloam" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 43|John 9:7]]). The man washes and comes back seeing. Excavations since 2004 have confirmed a large Second Temple-era pool at the southern end of the tunnel, with stepped sides for ritual bathing — the same Pool of Siloam that received Hezekiah's water and that Jesus later used as the site of one of his greatest healings.

**The Inscription's Language:** The Siloam Inscription is one of the longest and most important ancient Hebrew inscriptions ever found, written in the classical Biblical Hebrew of the 8th century BC — the very period of Isaiah, Micah, Amos, and Hosea. Its grammar, vocabulary, and script are consistent with the Hebrew of the Hebrew Bible from this period, providing direct linguistic corroboration of the textual tradition.

## Scholarly Discussion

The Siloam Inscription is universally accepted as authentic and dated to the 8th century BC with high confidence. The combination of paleographic analysis (letter forms), archaeological context, and carbon-14 dating all converge on the Hezekiah period. The identification with 2 Kings 20:20 and 2 Chronicles 32 is the scholarly consensus.

A separate Pool of Siloam was identified during road work in 2004 by archaeologists Ronny Reich and Eli Shukrun. The newly discovered pool — larger than the traditional Byzantine-era pool identified as Siloam for centuries — appears to be the Second Temple-period Pool of Siloam, complete with Second Temple-period stone steps and coins dating from the 1st century BC – 1st century AD. This makes it the most plausible identification of the pool in John 9.
