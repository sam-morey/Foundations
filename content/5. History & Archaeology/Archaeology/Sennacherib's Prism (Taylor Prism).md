**Current Location:** British Museum, London (Taylor Prism); Oriental Institute, University of Chicago (Oriental Institute Prism, also called the Sennacherib Prism)
**Biblical Connection:** 2 Kings 18–19; 2 Chronicles 32; Isaiah 36–37; the siege of Jerusalem in 701 BC

## The Discovery

In 1830, Colonel Robert Taylor, the British East India Company's representative in Baghdad, acquired a hexagonal clay prism approximately 38 cm tall inscribed with eight columns of dense Assyrian cuneiform text. The prism had been found in the ruins of Nineveh — the ancient capital of the Assyrian Empire, near modern Mosul in northern Iraq — and represented the annals of the Assyrian king Sennacherib (reigned 705–681 BC). Taylor donated the prism to the British Museum in 1855, where it remains one of the most visited objects in the ancient Near East collection.

Subsequent excavations in the 19th and early 20th centuries uncovered additional clay prisms with substantially the same content: the "Rassam Prism" (found by Hormuzd Rassam in 1853, now in the British Museum) and the "Oriental Institute Prism" (found in 1919 by the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago, now in Chicago). All three preserve Sennacherib's own account of his military campaigns, including his Third Campaign against Judah in 701 BC.

---

## What It Says

The prism records six military campaigns of Sennacherib in annalistic form — year by year accounts in the tradition of Assyrian royal inscription. The Third Campaign describes the invasion of Judah and its culmination at Jerusalem. The critical passage (in translation from the Assyrian cuneiform):

> "As for Hezekiah the Judahite, who did not submit to my yoke — 46 of his strong, walled cities, as well as the small towns in their area, which were without number, by leveling with battering-rams and by bringing up siege-engines, and by attacking and storming on foot, by mines, tunnels and breaches, I besieged and took [them]. 200,150 people, young and old, male and female, horses, mules, donkeys, camels, cattle and sheep without number, I brought away from them and counted as spoil. Himself I shut up like a bird in a cage in Jerusalem, his royal city. I threw up earthworks against him — the one coming out of the city gate, I turned back to his misery."

The passage continues to describe the tribute Hezekiah sent: gold, silver, precious stones, his daughters, his harem, musicians — a list that matches the tribute described in 2 Kings 18:14–16 with remarkable precision.

**The critical absence:** Sennacherib does NOT claim to have captured Jerusalem. For a king who celebrates the destruction of 46 fortified cities in Judah, the silence about Jerusalem's fall is deafening. The standard Assyrian formula — "[I took the city and] carried its people into exile" — is conspicuously absent. Sennacherib says he "shut up Hezekiah like a bird in a cage" — but a bird in a cage is not a bird that has been caught and killed.

## Biblical Significance

The Sennacherib Prism is one of the most powerful examples of an archaeological artifact corroborating a biblical narrative — not through what it says, but through what it does not say.

**What the prism confirms:**
- Sennacherib invaded Judah and besieged 46 cities — consistent with the biblical account of his campaign (2 Kings 18:13: "In the fourteenth year of King Hezekiah, Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the fortified cities of Judah and took them")
- Hezekiah was a real king of Judah, referred to by name
- Hezekiah paid heavy tribute — consistent with 2 Kings 18:14–16
- Jerusalem was besieged — consistent with 2 Kings 18–19

**What the prism conspicuously omits:**
- Any claim to have captured Jerusalem
- Any claim to have killed or captured or deported Hezekiah

The Bible's account explains the silence precisely. Second Kings 19:35–36 records: "And that night the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians. And when people arose early in the morning, behold, these were all dead bodies. Then Sennacherib king of Assyria departed and went home and lived at Nineveh" ([[2. The Bible (ESV)/12. II Kings/II Kings 19|2 Kings 19:35–36]]). Sennacherib left without taking Jerusalem — and his own annals, boastful as they are everywhere else, cannot claim otherwise.

This is among the most compelling arguments from archaeology for the historical accuracy of the biblical account. An Assyrian king who took 46 Judean cities and extracted massive tribute from Hezekiah would not have simply forgotten to mention taking Jerusalem if he had done so. The silence is the confirmation.

The Greek historian Herodotus (2.141) also records a tradition that Sennacherib's army was defeated in Egypt by a plague of mice that ate their bowstrings and quivers — a garbled version, perhaps, of a divine destruction of the Assyrian army. The corroborating traditions from multiple cultures converge on the same historical reality: Sennacherib's army was catastrophically defeated before or during the Jerusalem siege.

Sennacherib himself was later assassinated by his own sons while worshipping in the temple of his god Nisroch — exactly as 2 Kings 19:37 and Isaiah 37:38 predict.

## Scholarly Discussion

The Taylor Prism's authenticity is not disputed. It is one of the best-documented ancient Near Eastern royal inscriptions, with multiple exemplars and centuries of Assyrian cuneiform scholarship behind its translation.

The major scholarly discussion concerns the "200,150 people" figure. This number — if taken as a literal captive count — would represent a significant fraction of Judah's entire population. Most scholars read it as hyperbolic, in the tradition of ancient Near Eastern royal inscriptions that routinely exaggerated military achievements. The discrepancy between the prism's large numbers and the archaeological evidence of Judah's population is one of the ongoing discussions in Iron Age Levantine studies.

The correspondence between the tribute list in the prism and the tribute list in 2 Kings 18:14–16 (both mention gold, silver, and various luxury goods, though the numbers differ slightly) is considered strong evidence for the underlying historicity of both accounts, each drawing on the same historical events from their respective national perspectives.
