# Ezekiel

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** The Exile
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/26. Ezekiel Overview|Ezekiel]]

## Who They Are
Ezekiel is a priest-turned-prophet who ministers among the Jewish exiles in Babylon during the same period as Jeremiah's ministry in Jerusalem. He is given the most visually elaborate visions in all of the Old Testament — the living creatures and the chariot-throne, the valley of dry bones, and the vision of a restored Temple with a river flowing from its threshold. His central message is about the glory of God: it departed from the Temple because of Israel's sin, but it will return.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Ezekiel is the prophet of God's glory and the Holy Spirit. His two foundational truths — that the holiness of God cannot coexist with unrepentant sin (the departure of the glory) and that God will give a new heart and his own Spirit to renew his people — are foundational to both the theology of the cross and the theology of Pentecost.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Ezekiel 36–37 predicts the new covenant transformation of the human heart and the restoration of Israel — language that flows into Jesus's conversation with Nicodemus (John 3) and Paul's theology of the Spirit (Romans 8).
- **Legacy:** Ezekiel's imagery of the dry bones coming to life, the shepherd seeking lost sheep, and the Spirit-given heart are among the most powerful and enduring metaphors in all of Scripture. His vision of the new Temple and the life-giving river foreshadows the new creation.

## Character & Legacy
Ezekiel's ministry is characterized by its bodily, visceral nature. He is told to lie on his side for 390 days as a sign, to eat bread baked over dung, to shave his head, and to watch his wife die without mourning as a sign to Israel. His entire life becomes a living parable. He does not resist these impossible demands; he obeys. He is perhaps the most radical embodied prophet in the Old Testament, a man whose flesh becomes the medium of God's message.

His visions are simultaneously terrifying and beautiful. The chariot-throne (the Merkabah) is so overwhelming that he falls on his face again and again. He sees glory no one else has seen, and he sees the departure of that glory — a moment of such theological weight that the whole book's structure is built around its departure (chapters 10–11) and eventual return (chapter 43). His hope is not wishful thinking; it is grounded in what he has seen of God's character.

## New Testament Connections
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/40. Matthew/Matthew 18|Matthew 18:12–14]] — Jesus's parable of the lost sheep echoes Ezekiel 34's promise that God himself will seek the lost
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/42. Luke/Luke 15|Luke 15:3–7]] — The seeking shepherd of Ezekiel 34 finds its fulfillment in Jesus, the Good Shepherd
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/40. Matthew/Matthew 3|Matthew 3:16]] — The Spirit descending at Jesus's baptism begins the fulfillment of Ezekiel 36:27, "I will put my Spirit within you"
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/42. Luke/Luke 3|Luke 3:5]] — John the Baptist quotes from the great leveling of valleys from the same prophetic stream Ezekiel represents
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/58. Hebrews/Hebrews 9|Hebrews 9:11–14]] — The new temple Ezekiel envisioned is fulfilled in Christ himself as the new meeting place between God and humanity
