## Who They Are
Rebekah is the wife of Isaac and the mother of Esau and Jacob, sought out by Abraham's servant through an act of faithful providence and chosen by God before her twins were born. She is one of the most active and purposeful women in Genesis, playing a decisive role in ensuring the covenant blessing passes to Jacob. Her story is marked by faith, initiative, and a troubling willingness to deceive in order to accomplish what she believed was God's will.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Rebekah's story is used by Paul in Romans 9 to illustrate the doctrine of sovereign election. Before her twins had done anything good or evil, God declared that the elder would serve the younger — showing that God's choice rests on his own purpose, not human merit.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Without Rebekah's intervention — however morally compromised — Jacob receives the blessing and the covenant continues. God uses her decisive action, even her deception, within his sovereign plan.
- **Legacy:** Rebekah is remembered as one who heard the promise directly from God and acted on it — but whose methods raise enduring questions about whether the right ends ever justify deceptive means, and about what it costs to take God's plan into one's own hands.

## Character & Legacy
Rebekah is characterized from her first appearance by energy and generosity. When Abraham's servant arrives at the well, she doesn't just draw water for him — she offers to water all his camels, a backbreaking act of hospitality. When asked if she will go with the servant, she says simply, "I will go" — a word of faith not unlike Abraham's own departure from Ur.

Her later manipulation of Isaac is harder to admire. She overheard Isaac's plan to bless Esau, and devised the deception herself, instructing Jacob step by step. She even takes the curse upon herself if it goes wrong. There is something bold and even sacrificial in her action, but it initiates a rupture in the family from which Jacob must flee and to which he will not return. Rebekah, who did everything to secure her favored son's future, apparently never sees Jacob again. The text leaves her legacy beautifully and painfully ambiguous.
