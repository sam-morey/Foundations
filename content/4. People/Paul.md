# Paul

**Testament:** New Testament
**Era:** The Early Church
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/44. Acts Overview|Acts]], [[3. Overviews/45. Romans Overview|Romans]], [[3. Overviews/46-57. Paul's Letters Overview|Paul's Letters]]

## Who He Is
Saul of Tarsus — known after his conversion as Paul — is the former Pharisee and fierce persecutor of the church who became its greatest missionary and theologian. His encounter with the risen Jesus on the road to Damascus was the most dramatic conversion in Christian history, and it reoriented his entire life: the man who dragged Christians to prison became the man who sang hymns in prison. Commissioned as the apostle to the Gentiles (Galatians 1:16), his three missionary journeys carried the gospel across the Roman world, and his thirteen letters form the theological backbone of the New Testament.

## Why He Matters
- His conversion is a standing proof of the resurrection: no adequate explanation exists for his transformation except that he truly encountered the risen Jesus
- His letters articulate the meaning of the cross, justification by faith, life in the Spirit, the nature of the church, and the shape of the new creation with a depth and precision unmatched in the NT
- As the apostle to the Gentiles, he is the primary human instrument through whom the gospel crossed ethnic and geographic boundaries to reach the entire Roman world — and, through that, the whole world

## Character & Legacy
Paul was many things at once: a trained Pharisee with command of the Hebrew Scriptures, a Roman citizen with the legal protections that afforded, a tentmaker who funded his own ministry, and a man of extraordinary personal courage who was flogged, shipwrecked, stoned, and imprisoned for the gospel (2 Corinthians 11:23–28). He was not an easy person — he argued with Peter publicly over the truth of the gospel (Galatians 2:11), he parted company sharply with Barnabas over John Mark, and his letters show a man who could be tender and fierce within the same paragraph.

Yet the man who wrote "I have learned, in whatever situation I am, to be content" (Philippians 4:11) from a prison cell, and who declared "to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Philippians 1:21), was a man in whom the gospel had done its deep work. Tradition holds that he was ultimately beheaded in Rome under Nero — dying, like his Lord, at the hands of the empire he had once served.
