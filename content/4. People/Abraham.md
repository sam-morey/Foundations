# Abraham

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** Patriarchal Age
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/01. Genesis Overview|Genesis]]

## Who They Are
Abraham (originally Abram) is the father of the Jewish people and the foundational figure of biblical faith. Called out of Ur of the Chaldeans, he follows God's voice to an unknown land, receiving a covenant promise that through his offspring all the nations of the earth would be blessed. He is the paradigmatic man of faith — justified not by works but by belief in God's promise — and his willingness to offer Isaac prefigures the Father's sacrifice of his own Son.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Abraham's justification by faith (Genesis 15:6) is the cornerstone of Paul's argument in Romans and Galatians that salvation has always been by grace through faith, not law-keeping. He proves that God's people are defined by faith, not ethnicity.
- **Role in redemptive history:** The Abrahamic covenant is the backbone of the entire Old Testament. Every subsequent act of God — the Exodus, the Davidic kingdom, the prophets, and finally Christ — is an unfolding of the promise made to Abraham: land, seed, and blessing to all nations.
- **Legacy:** Paul calls him "the father of us all" (Romans 4:16) — the spiritual father of all who believe, Jew and Gentile alike. His willingness to sacrifice Isaac is treated in Hebrews 11 as the supreme act of faith in the Old Testament.

## Character & Legacy
Abraham is a man of genuine faith who nonetheless stumbles repeatedly. He twice passes Sarah off as his sister out of fear (Genesis 12, 20). He sleeps with Hagar when the promise seems impossible, producing Ishmael and a fractured family legacy. He laughs at God's promise of a son in his old age. These are not footnotes — they are part of the portrait. His faith is real but imperfect, growing over decades through tests and failures.

What distinguishes Abraham is that he keeps believing despite impossibility. Romans 4 notes that he did not weaken in faith when his body was "as good as dead," but grew stronger in faith, giving glory to God. The climax of his story is Genesis 22, where he raises the knife over Isaac with the conviction that God could raise the dead. In this act, the entire logic of the gospel — a father giving his beloved son, a son received back from death — is acted out on Mount Moriah, the very hill where Jerusalem and the Temple would stand.
