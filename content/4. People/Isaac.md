# Isaac

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** Patriarchal Age
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/01. Genesis Overview|Genesis]]

## Who They Are
Isaac is the son of promise, the child born to Abraham and Sarah in their extreme old age through the miraculous power of God. He is the second patriarch of Israel, carrying the Abrahamic covenant forward to Jacob. His near-sacrifice on Mount Moriah is the defining episode of his story — and one of the most theologically dense moments in all of Scripture. He is in many ways the quietest of the patriarchs, but the covenant thread runs through him just as surely.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Isaac's very existence is the product of resurrection-like power — life from what was dead. His binding on the altar (the Akedah) foreshadows the sacrifice of God's own Son so directly that Hebrews 11:19 says Abraham "received him back" as a type, reasoning that God could raise the dead.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Isaac is the indispensable link in the covenant chain. Without him there is no Jacob, no twelve tribes, no people of Israel, no Messiah. His life is sustained not by his own strength or cleverness but by God's faithfulness alone.
- **Legacy:** Paul explicitly calls believers "children of promise like Isaac" (Galatians 4:28), meaning Isaac's identity as the miraculous, grace-given son of promise is the template for every Christian's identity before God.

## Character & Legacy
Isaac is the most passive of the patriarchs. He is carried to the altar, he receives a wife chosen for him by his father's servant, he re-digs rather than digs new wells, and he is deceived in his old age. His most dramatic scene — Genesis 22 — says almost nothing about his own thoughts or reactions. Yet this passivity is not weakness: it is a kind of trust. He goes to the altar carrying the wood for his own sacrifice, asking his father where the lamb is, and apparently submitting to being bound.

The deception of Genesis 27 reveals his limitations as a father: he favors Esau despite God's word to Rebekah that the elder would serve the younger. Yet even his misguided blessing of Jacob cannot thwart God's purpose — he trembles at the realization that God has overruled his intentions and confirms that Jacob will indeed be blessed. In this, God's sovereign grace triumphs over human partiality.
