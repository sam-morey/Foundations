# Samson

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** The Judges
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/07. Judges Overview|Judges]]

## Who They Are
Samson is a Nazirite judge of Israel, set apart from birth to begin Israel's deliverance from Philistine oppression. His supernatural physical strength comes from God and is connected to the terms of his Nazirite vow — including the prohibition against cutting his hair. He is the most individualistic of all the judges, and his story is a long, tragic account of personal gifting squandered through self-indulgence and a fatal weakness for foreign women. His greatest act of deliverance comes in his death — pulling down the temple of Dagon, killing more Philistines in one moment than in all his life — making him a flawed but genuine pointer to the One who defeats the enemy through his own death.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Samson's story is the book of Judges' most extreme portrait of a truth it has been developing throughout: Israel's judges are as spiritually compromised as the people they deliver. Samson embodies Israel itself — gifted, chosen, called, and perpetually drawn toward the idolatries and alliances God has forbidden. His story is not an aberration but a mirror.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Samson "began to deliver Israel" from the Philistines (Judges 13:5) — begun but not completed. His death-deliverance points forward to the greater Judge-King who would not merely begin the deliverance but complete it, not by killing enemies but by dying for them.
- **Legacy:** Hebrews 11 lists Samson among the heroes of faith — a seemingly astonishing placement that makes sense when you see that his final act (prayer, dependence on God, willing death that achieves salvation for others) is genuinely faithful in a way his life rarely was. He is remembered as a warning and a type: a flawed deliverer who points to the need for a better one.

## Character & Legacy
Samson is gifted with extraordinary strength and hamstrung by extraordinary self-will. He violates his Nazirite vow repeatedly — touching a dead body, feasting at a Philistine wedding — and pursues relationships with Philistine women against his parents' protests and God's design. The Spirit of God comes upon him powerfully, but Samson uses this power for personal revenge as often as for Israel's deliverance. He is reactive, impulsive, and always seems to be fighting for himself rather than for God's people.

The Delilah episode is painful precisely because it is so unnecessary. She asks him the secret of his strength four times, and three times he gives false answers while she has the Philistines waiting. He cannot resist the nagging — not because he is naïve but because he simply will not exercise self-control. When he finally tells the truth and wakes to find his strength gone, the text says something devastating: "he did not know that the Lord had left him." He had grown so accustomed to God's grace that he stopped noticing it.

And yet his last prayer — "Lord God, remember me and please strengthen me only this once" — is one of the most human prayers in Scripture. He asks for strength not to escape but to die in the service of deliverance. God answers. Samson is the worst best example: a man through whom God accomplished salvation despite his unfaithfulness, not because of it. In this he is the most honest portrait of Israel — and perhaps, uncomfortably, of every believer who has squandered gifts and returned to God only in extremity.

## New Testament Connections
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/58. Hebrews/Hebrews 11|Hebrews 11:32]] — Samson commended for faith; his final act qualifies him for the Hall of Faith
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 12|John 12:24]] — "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies..." — the death-that-defeats principle Samson embodies is fulfilled in Christ
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/46. 1 Corinthians/1 Corinthians 1|1 Corinthians 1:25]] — God's weakness is stronger than human strength — Samson's story ends with God's power through a broken, humbled vessel
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/47. 2 Corinthians/2 Corinthians 12|2 Corinthians 12:9]] — Strength made perfect in weakness; Samson's final act illustrates this principle before its NT articulation
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/45. Romans/Romans 5|Romans 5:8]] — Christ died for the ungodly; Samson's saving death for a people who did not deserve it is a shadow of this greater reality
