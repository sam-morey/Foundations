# Rahab

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** The Conquest
**Appears In:** [[3. Overviews/06. Joshua Overview|Joshua]]

## Who They Are
Rahab is a Canaanite prostitute living in Jericho who hides the Israelite spies sent by Joshua before the conquest of the city. In exchange for her protection of the spies, she is given a promise of salvation — her household will be spared when Jericho falls, marked by a scarlet cord hung in her window. She is one of the most surprising figures in the Old Testament: an outsider, a Gentile, a woman of questionable reputation who becomes a hero of faith, appears in Jesus's genealogy, and is named in Hebrews 11.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Rahab's story demolishes every assumption about who belongs among God's people. She is Canaanite, female, and a prostitute — excluded by nearly every social and religious category of her day — and yet her faith-driven action saves her. Grace reaches across every boundary.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Rahab is not a footnote. She is woven into the genealogy of Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5), becoming the mother of Boaz and thus the great-great-grandmother of David. The line of the Messiah runs through this Canaanite woman of faith.
- **Legacy:** The scarlet cord hung from her window has been read by early Christian interpreters as a type of the blood of Christ — the mark that causes death to pass over. Whether or not one presses that typology, Rahab's salvation through a sign of red on her house does carry a Passover resonance.

## Character & Legacy
Rahab's confession in Joshua 2 is one of the most striking declarations of faith in the Old Testament. Before Israel has set foot across the Jordan, a Canaanite woman says: "I know that the Lord has given you the land... the Lord your God, he is God in the heavens above and on the earth beneath." This is more explicit than much of what Israel itself said during the wilderness period. Her faith is not theoretical — she risks her life to act on it.

James cites her as an example of faith demonstrated by works, alongside Abraham. This pairing is intentional and powerful: the patriarch Abraham and the Canaanite prostitute Rahab stand as the two poles of biblical faith in action — one is Israel's founding father, one is a Gentile outsider; one is male, one is female; one is the most exalted figure in Israel's history, one is among the most marginalized. Together they define the scope of saving faith. Rahab's place in Jesus's genealogy ensures that her story is never merely illustrative — it is structural to the story of redemption.
