# Joseph

**Testament:** Old Testament
**Era:** Patriarchal Age
**Appears In:** [[3. Book Overviews/01. Genesis Overview|Genesis]]

## Who They Are
Joseph is the eleventh son of Jacob and the beloved son of Rachel, whose story spans the final fourteen chapters of Genesis. Sold into slavery by his jealous brothers, falsely imprisoned, and then raised to the second-highest seat in Egypt, Joseph becomes the instrument of salvation for his family and for the nations. He is the richest type of Christ in Genesis — the suffering servant who is rejected by his brothers, exalted by God, and becomes the source of life for those who wronged him.

## Why They Matter
- **Theological significance:** Joseph's declaration in Genesis 50:20 — "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" — is one of the clearest statements of divine providence in the Bible. Suffering is not outside God's sovereignty; it is woven into his redemptive purpose.
- **Role in redemptive history:** Joseph's descent into Egypt and subsequent exaltation sets the stage for the entire Exodus narrative. His story also foreshadows the pattern of the rejected-and-exalted savior that reaches its fullest expression in the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ.
- **Legacy:** Stephen in Acts 7 identifies Joseph as the first in a pattern of deliverers whom Israel rejected but God vindicated. Joseph is the lens through which the New Testament understands the apparent scandal of a rejected Messiah.

## Character & Legacy
Joseph is gifted, favored, and — in his youth — perhaps a little oblivious to how his recounting of dreams affects his brothers. He is not morally perfect but he is remarkably consistent: at every low point (Potiphar's house, prison), the text repeats that "the Lord was with Joseph." His integrity under Potiphar's wife, his refusal to compromise despite years of unjust imprisonment, and his attribution of his gift of interpretation to God alone paint a portrait of a man whose identity is rooted in God's presence, not his circumstances.

His forgiveness of his brothers is not naïve sentimentality — it comes after years of testing them to see if they have changed. When he finally reveals himself, he weeps loudly enough that the Egyptians outside can hear. This is not controlled diplomacy; it is the flood of emotion from a man who has held grief and love together for decades. His final word — "You meant evil; God meant it for good, to bring about that many people should be kept alive" — is not bitterness resolved but theology arrived at through suffering. It is the voice of a man who truly believes God writes with crooked lines.

## New Testament Connections
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/44. Acts/Acts 7|Acts 7:9–16]] — Stephen uses Joseph as a type of the rejected-then-exalted deliverer, paralleling Jesus
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/58. Hebrews/Hebrews 11|Hebrews 11:22]] — Joseph's faith evidenced by his confidence in the coming Exodus
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/43. John/John 1|John 1:11]] — "He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him" — the Josephic pattern applied to Jesus
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/50. Philippians/Philippians 2|Philippians 2:8–9]] — The pattern of humiliation then exaltation mirrors Joseph's trajectory and is fulfilled in Christ
- [[2. The Bible (ESV)/45. Romans/Romans 8|Romans 8:28]] — "All things work together for good" is the New Testament distillation of Genesis 50:20
